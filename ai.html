AI

How to use AI in media work responsibly and legally: what to keep out of it, who owns what it makes, what you can't make with it, and when to say you used it.

This page explains US law and industry practice in plain terms. It isn't legal advice. The law on AI is changing quickly. Each class sets its own AI rules, and some assignments allow no AI at all. The class rules come first.

The SJMC approach

AI is a tool, and professionals use it differently than beginners do. Beginners ask it to do the work. Professionals bring the ideas and the judgment, and use AI to speed up production.

  1. Start on your own. Learn the concepts and do the first thinking yourself: the story, the angle, the audience, the idea. Never start with AI.
  2. Use AI for production work, not to do the work. Once you have your own ideas, you can test them against AI, and use it for simple production tasks: cleaning up audio, removing a background, writing code for a layout you designed, transcribing an interview.
  3. Come back in at the end. Check, edit, and refine everything the AI made. You're responsible for all of it.

The test: can you explain and defend every choice in the work with the AI closed? If not, you don't understand the work well enough to publish it.

Assisting your skills or replacing them

Professional useSurface-level use
The tool speeds up skills you already have.The tool fills in for knowledge you don't have.
You judge, edit, and override what it makes.You accept what it makes with little review.
Your skills and judgment lead. The software assists.The software leads.
Speed lets you do more.Speed hides what you don't understand.

This applies to templates and tools like Canva as much as to AI.

Before AI text goes into your work

  • Accuracy: Have you checked every claim against a real source? See Accuracy.
  • Understanding: Could you explain and defend it without the AI?
  • Editorial control: Did you decide what belongs and what matters most, or did you accept what it gave you?
  • Fit: Does it suit the audience, purpose, medium, and tone? AI defaults to generic, overly polished writing. See AI slop.

Kinds of AI tools

KindExamplesWhat to watch for
Machine learning utilitiesFeatures built into software that do one task: background removal, noise reduction, transcription, auto-captions, subject selection, Enhance SpeechThey save time on tasks you could do by hand. Check the result. Transcripts and captions get names and words wrong.
Generative fill and upscalingPhotoshop Generative Fill, AI upscaling, AI image expansionThey invent pixels that weren't there. Not allowed in news photos. See Photo: Editing standards.
Broad generative AIChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot. One tool that makes text, images, code, and more from a prompt.Fluent and confident, and can be wrong. Not a search engine. See Research. Code it writes needs reading and testing. See Web: Code written by AI.
Media-specific generative AITools built for one medium: Adobe Firefly and Midjourney for images, video generators, voice and music generatorsThey make realistic media of things that never happened. The rules on this page apply most to these, and to images and video from broad generative AI.

AI upscaling is sold as a utility, but it's generative. To add detail, it guesses from other images it has seen, not from this one, so it changes what's in the picture. Never use it where the image has to show what really happened.

Models and platforms

Generative AI runs on models: versions trained on different data, with different strengths. One may be better at text inside images, another at painterly illustration, another at facts. You use a model through a platform, such as ChatGPT or Gemini, which may offer several of its company's models. Other platforms let you choose models from several companies.

Models change every few months. Part of using AI professionally is knowing which current model fits the job, and checking again later. Free versions usually limit which models you get and how much you can make. See Software.

Privacy and confidential material

What you type or upload into an AI tool goes to the company that runs it. Depending on the tool and your settings, it can be stored, read by staff, or used to train future models.

Don't upload

  • Names or details of confidential sources
  • Unpublished interviews, recordings, notes, and drafts of stories
  • Client material: plans, data, unreleased products, anything under a nondisclosure agreement
  • Other students' work, grades, or personal information
  • Photos, video, or voice recordings of people who haven't agreed to it
  • Passwords, account numbers, and personal ID numbers

Before you use a tool

  • Check its data settings. Many let you turn off training on your conversations.
  • Use the university's or employer's approved tools for work material when they exist. These usually come with an agreement that protects the data.
  • When in doubt, ask the source, client, or editor before their material goes into an AI tool.

Accuracy

Generative AI and transcription tools produce errors that look correct: invented facts, quotes, sources, and statistics; misspelled names; wrong dates. The more fluent the text, the easier it is to miss.

Terms to know

TermWhat it means
AccuracyHow often the AI gives a correct answer
HallucinationMaking up false information instead of saying "I don't know"
BluffingStating something made up with complete confidence
Retrieval groundingHow closely the AI sticks to the sources it pulled when it searches. See Research: When AI searches the web.
Citation integrityWhether its sources are real and say what it claims
Model divergenceDifferent models get different things wrong

How often it's wrong

Different models have different accuracy and hallucination rates, and each does better at some tasks than others. The rates change as models are updated. Suprmind keeps a current comparison: AI hallucination rates and benchmarks.

Studies of news and research tasks have found high error rates:

  • 45% of AI answers about the news had at least one significant problem. Sourcing problems were the most common, in 31%. (European Broadcasting Union and BBC, October 2025. Journalists at 22 public media organizations checked more than 3,000 answers.)
  • More than 60% of AI search tools' answers were wrong when asked to find the news article a quotation came from. (Tow Center for Digital Journalism, Columbia University, March 2025)
  • 11% to 57% of academic citations were made up, depending on the model. (Naser, 2026, a preprint not yet peer reviewed)

Tools improve and tests differ, so don't treat these as fixed rates. They're a reason to check.

Checking with more than one model

Because models get different things wrong, running the same question through two or three can expose errors. Agreement isn't proof, though. Models can learn the same mistakes from the same data. Checking a real source comes first.

You're responsible

In 2023, lawyers in New York filed a court brief that cited six cases ChatGPT had made up. The court fined them $5,000. The judge said using AI wasn't the problem. Not checking it was. (Mata v. Avianca, U.S. District Court, Southern District of New York, 2023)

Your name goes on your story, your paper, and your client's campaign. If AI made it up, it's your error.

Checking

  • Check every fact against a real source you've opened and read. See Research.
  • Open every source the AI gives you and check that it exists and says what the AI claims. See Research: Checking a source AI gives you.
  • Check every quote against the recording.
  • Check names, titles, and places against an official source.

AI slop

AI slop is AI-made media published without enough human judgment: text, images, graphics, and pages that look finished but are generic, padded, or wrong. It's easy to make a lot of it, so there's a lot of it.

Audiences have learned to spot it, and they scroll past it or stop trusting the source. In journalism it costs credibility. In strategic communication it makes a brand look like every other brand. Either way, it doesn't earn attention.

The signs below are clues, not proof. People used dashes and lists of three long before AI. Use them to check your own work, not to accuse someone else.

Text

  • Filler openings and closings: "In today's fast-paced world," "Let's dive in," "In conclusion," "Ultimately."
  • Inflated words: delve, elevate, unlock, seamless, robust, vibrant, landscape, tapestry, testament, game-changer.
  • Set patterns: "It's not just X, it's Y." Lists of three with no facts in them: "fun, affordable, and unforgettable." Three is a good rhythm when each item says something: "Fireworks Fridays, $2 hot dogs Thursdays, kids free on Sundays." Questions the writer answers right away: "The result? A better experience."
  • No specifics: no names, numbers, places, or dates. The text could be about any team, town, or company.
  • Hedging: "It's important to note," "While there are many factors."
  • Too much structure: headings, bullets, and bold labels on everything, small labels above every heading, "Key takeaways" boxes. Structure that organizes nothing.
  • Even rhythm: sentences of similar length and shape, paragraph after paragraph.
  • On social media: emojis as bullet points, stacks of hashtags, and a hook question in every post.
SlopSpecific
In today's fast-paced world, the Kernels are elevating the fan experience to new heights.The Kernels opened a second gate this season. The line at first pitch dropped from 20 minutes to 5.
Birdwatching isn't just a hobby. It's a journey of discovery, connection, and wonder.Volunteers counted 84 species in Johnson County on Saturday, six more than last year.

Images

  • Too smooth and too perfect: plastic-looking skin, glossy surfaces, glowing light from no clear source, oversaturated color.
  • Details that fall apart up close: garbled text on signs and shirts, wrong numbers of fingers, earrings that don't match, objects that melt into each other, people in the background who blur into shapes.
  • Things that don't fit the place: mountains behind Iowa City, a uniform with the wrong colors, a bird that's no real species.
  • Generic stock look: centered subject, everyone smiling at the camera, a scene that could be anywhere.

Graphics and layouts

  • Overloaded: gradients, glows, drop shadows, 3D icons, and several fonts at once. When everything is emphasized, nothing is.
  • An icon for every point, whether or not it adds information.
  • Decoration that doesn't match the content: abstract shapes, stock illustrations of people at laptops, a color scheme picked by the tool, not the brand.
  • Infographics with made-up or meaningless numbers, charts with no data behind them, and labels that don't add up.

Websites

  • Organized but bland: the same section order as every template. A large opening section with a vague headline, three cards with icons, testimonials, a list of questions that open and close, a sign-up box.
  • Vague headlines: "Elevate your experience," "Where innovation meets community." They could be on any site.
  • Everything centered, in rounded boxes, with a small label, a heading, and a subheading for every section.
  • No real photos of the actual people, place, or product.

Video and audio

  • AI voices with flat, even delivery that mispronounce local names.
  • Generated footage where shapes shift and morph between frames.
  • Effects on everything: every word of the captions popping, zooming, and changing color.

Avoiding it

  • Start with your own idea and your own reporting or research. Specifics come from you, not the tool. See The SJMC approach.
  • Edit AI text line by line. Cut filler, replace vague words with names, numbers, and facts, and read it aloud.
  • Use real photos of the actual subject whenever you can.
  • Cut decoration that carries no information. Every element in a layout should help the reader.
  • Design for this audience and this content, not the template's.
  • The test: could this be about a different team, town, or company with only the name changed? If so, it isn't finished.

Copyright and ownership

  • AI output often can't be copyrighted. The US Copyright Office won't register material made entirely by AI. The human-made parts of a work, like your own writing, editing, or arrangement, can be protected. A client may not be able to own AI-made work the way they own work you made.
  • AI can copy. Image and text tools can produce material close to copyrighted characters, artwork, photos, logos, and writing. Asking for something "in the style of" a living artist makes this more likely. You're responsible for what you publish, not the tool.
  • Trademarks still apply. A logo or product in an AI image is still a trademark. See Trademarks.
  • Read the terms. Each tool's terms of service say who can use its output and how, and whether commercial use is allowed. Read them before using output in client work.
  • Training data is in court. Several lawsuits are deciding whether training AI on copyrighted work without permission is legal. Some tools, like Adobe Firefly, say they trained only on licensed material. Clients may ask which tool you used.
  • Uploading counts too. Uploading someone else's copyrighted work to an AI tool can itself be a problem, and many tools can use what you upload to train their models.
  • Your prompts can come out. In a lawsuit, prompt histories, chat logs, and messages can be requested as evidence. Don't claim work as fully your own when it isn't.

Logos

A logo has to be owned and protected, so it's where AI and template tools cause the most trouble.

  • A logo made by AI can't be copyrighted, so it's hard to stop someone else from using one just like it. A business that wants to register and defend a trademark needs a logo a person designed. AI can help with early mood boards.
  • AI material you rework: the parts you drew and arranged yourself are protected. The raw AI parts aren't.
  • Canva templates and stock graphics are licensed to everyone, so a logo built from them isn't yours alone, and Canva's license doesn't allow its stock graphics in a trademark. A logo made from basic shapes and standard fonts in your own layout is different. Check Canva's current terms.
  • The risk depends on the client. A student club's T-shirt logo carries little risk. A business that plans to grow, franchise, or register a trademark needs a logo it can own.

Rights for material you didn't make are on Rights and Credit.

Real people

Using AI to make a real person appear to say or do something they didn't is the highest-risk use of AI.

  • Don't make fake media of real people and present it as real. This includes faces, bodies, and voices.
  • Voice clones and digital likenesses need written consent, like a model release, that says exactly how they'll be used. Using someone's likeness to promote something without permission can break right-of-publicity laws.
  • Sexual or intimate images of a real person made with AI without their consent are illegal to publish under federal law, and in many states.
  • Political content: many states require labels on AI-made content in election ads and ban deceptive AI media of candidates close to an election.

Rules in journalism

Each newsroom has its own AI policy. Most share these rules:

  • Photos, video, and audio show what happened. Generative AI isn't used to add, remove, or change anything in them.
  • AI images are never presented as real. When an AI image is used, such as in a story about AI, it's labeled clearly as AI-made.
  • AI isn't a source. Facts, quotes, and statistics come from people and documents you verified.
  • A journalist is responsible for every word. AI-assisted writing, summaries, and translations are checked and edited by a person before publishing. Many newsrooms don't allow AI-written stories at all.
  • Unpublished material stays out of public AI tools. See Privacy.

Rules in strategic communication

  • Tell the client. Say which parts of the work used AI, and which tools. Some clients don't allow it, or require specific tools.
  • No fake reviews or testimonials. The Federal Trade Commission bans fake reviews, including ones written by AI, and endorsements from people who don't exist presented as real customers.
  • Choose illustration over photorealism. A photorealistic image tells viewers this scene happened or this thing exists. An illustration's materials and linework show it's a representation. In SJMC classes, don't use AI to make photorealistic images in most cases.
  • Ads can't mislead. An AI-made image that shows a product doing something it can't, or a result a customer won't get, is false advertising, the same as a retouched one.
  • Follow platform rules. Meta, TikTok, and YouTube require labels on realistic AI-made or AI-altered content. Their tools add the label when you post.
  • Brand voice is a choice. AI copy tends to sound like every other brand. Edit it into the client's voice, or write it yourself.

Disclosure and labels

When to disclose

  • When AI made or changed something the audience could take as real: a person, a place, an event, a voice.
  • When AI wrote a large part of the text.
  • When a class, newsroom, client, or platform requires it.

You usually don't need to disclose machine learning utilities that do production work a person could do by hand without changing the content, like noise reduction or transcription you checked.

How to word it

Say what the AI did, plainly, where the audience will see it: in the caption, the credit line, or a note at the end.

  • "Illustration made with Adobe Firefly and edited by Jordan Lee."
  • "This summary was drafted with AI and checked by an editor."
  • "Narration is an AI-generated voice used with the speaker's permission."

Content credentials

Content credentials (the C2PA standard) are information attached to a file that records how it was made and edited, including whether AI was used. Adobe apps, some cameras, and some AI tools add them, and some platforms read them to show a label. Exporting, screenshotting, or uploading to some platforms can strip them, so they don't replace a written disclosure.

Where to find it: In Photoshop, Window › Content Credentials. In the export dialog, choose whether to attach them.

Quick answers

Can I use AI on this assignment?
Check the class and assignment rules first. They come before this page. See The SJMC approach.
Can I paste my interview transcript into ChatGPT or another generative AI tool?
Not into a public tool if it's unpublished or from a confidential source. Use an approved tool, or ask. See Privacy.
Can I use an AI image in a news story?
Only when AI is part of the story, and labeled clearly. Never as a stand-in for a real photo. See Rules in journalism.
Which AI tool is most accurate?
It depends on the task, and it changes as models are updated. Check a current comparison, and check the facts either way. See Accuracy.
Can I make a client's logo with AI?
For mood boards, yes. The final logo should be designed by a person if the client needs to own it. See Copyright and ownership.
Does my client own the AI images I made for them?
Maybe not. Material made entirely by AI can't be copyrighted. See Copyright and ownership.
Can I clone someone's voice for narration?
Only with their written permission for that use. See Real people.
How do I know if my work looks like AI slop?
Check whether it could be about any team, town, or company with only the name changed. Look for filler, vague words, and decoration with no purpose. See AI slop.
Do I have to say I used AI?
When it made or changed something the audience could take as real, wrote much of the text, or a class, client, or platform requires it. See Disclosure and labels.
