Photo Technical Basics

How to control a camera instead of letting it decide: exposure, shooting modes, ISO, focus, focal length, camera files, metadata, editing standards, and noise reduction and sharpening.

Resolution, color, and print basics are on Graphics. Using other people's photos and model releases are on Rights and Credit.

Exposure

Exposure is how much light makes up the photo. Three settings control it. Each one also changes how the photo looks.

SettingWhat it controlsWhat else it changes
Aperture (f-stop)How wide the lens opensDepth of field: how much is in focus from front to back
Shutter speedHow long the sensor is exposedMotion: frozen or blurred
ISOHow much the camera brightens the signalNoise: grain and loss of detail

Change one and you have to change another to keep the same brightness. A faster shutter to freeze a runner lets in less light, so you open the aperture or raise the ISO.

Aperture

Aperture numbers work backwards: a low number is a wide opening.

ApertureLightDepth of fieldUse it for
f/1.4–f/2.8MostVery shallow. Background blurred.Portraits, low light, separating a subject from a busy background
f/4–f/5.6MediumModerateSmall groups, events, general news
f/8–f/11LessDeep. Most of the scene sharp.Landscapes, buildings, large groups
f/16 and upLeastDeepestRarely needed. Photos start to get softer overall.

Shutter speed

Shutter speedUse it for
1/2000 and fasterBirds in flight, fast sports
1/500–1/1000Most sports, running kids, moving cars
1/250People walking, general events
1/60–1/125Still subjects, handheld, with a short lens
Slower than 1/60Tripod, or blur on purpose: moving water, light trails

To avoid blur from your own hands, keep the shutter speed at least 1 over the lens's focal length. At 200 mm, use 1/200 or faster. Image stabilization lets you go a little slower. It doesn't freeze a moving subject.

ISO

ISO brightens the image after the light hits the sensor. Higher ISO lets you shoot in less light, but adds noise: grain, blotchy color, and lost fine detail. Set aperture and shutter speed first for the photo you want, then raise ISO only as far as you need.

Which ISO to use:

ISOLightNoise
100–200Bright sun, studio lights, tripodNone. The cleanest the camera can make.
400Overcast, open shade, bright rooms with big windowsVery little
800–1600Indoors, evening, well-lit gyms and stagesSome, fine for most uses
3200–6400Dim rooms, night events, indoor sportsVisible. Fine for news.
12800 and upVery dark scenesHeavy. Last resort.

These are for typical interchangeable-lens cameras. Phones and small cameras get noisy at lower ISOs. Newer full-frame cameras stay clean at higher ones. Test yours.

A sharp photo with noise is better than a clean photo that's blurry. Noise can be reduced in editing. Blur can't be fixed. See Noise reduction and sharpening.

Auto ISO

Auto ISO lets the camera change the ISO for every frame. Turn it off.

  • It takes away manual control. In manual mode with auto ISO on, the camera adjusts brightness for you, so you're shooting in an automatic mode without knowing it.
  • It raises ISO without telling you. The camera will go to ISO 12800 before it tells you the light is too low. You find the noise later, on the computer.
  • It makes a series inconsistent. Frames from the same scene come out at different brightness and noise levels.

Auto ISO is on by default on many cameras. Check for it in the ISO menu before every shoot.

Understanding the reciprocal nature of exposure

A scene has one amount of light, measured as an exposure value (EV). Many combinations of aperture, shutter speed, and ISO give the same correct exposure for it. In this demo the exposure always stays correct: when you change one setting, another changes the opposite way. The setting you changed last stays where you put it. If the setting that adjusts reaches its limit, the third one takes over. Watch what each trade does to the photo.

Histograms and clipping

The camera's screen is a poor guide to exposure, especially outdoors. Use the histogram.

Reading a histogram

A histogram is a graph of every pixel's brightness. Dark tones are on the left, bright tones on the right. The height shows how many pixels have that brightness.

There's no correct shape. A snowy field is piled to the right, and a night street to the left. What matters is the edges.

Clipping

When the graph runs into either edge, those pixels are clipped: pure black on the left, pure white on the right. They hold no detail, and editing can't add it back.

Turn on the camera's highlight warning, sometimes called blinkies. Clipped areas blink on playback, so you can see exactly what's lost.

Highlights or shadows: which to protect

When you can't keep both, protect the highlights in most cases.

  • Highlights stop suddenly. A sensor's pixels can only hold so much light. Past that point, the detail is gone. Clipped highlights show as flat white patches, sometimes with odd color at the edges.
  • Shadows fade gradually. Dark areas still hold detail, mixed with noise. Brightening them brings the detail back, with more noise.
  • People accept dark more easily than white. Deep shadows look natural. A blown-out sky or face looks like a mistake.

It isn't always the right call. Expose for what matters in the photo:

  • Small bright spots can clip. The sun, lamps, and reflections off water or metal are expected to be white.
  • A subject in shadow comes first. For a person in front of a bright window, expose for the face and let the window go white, or add light to the face.
  • Silhouettes clip the shadows on purpose.

What RAW can rescue

The histogram and highlight warning on the camera come from a JPG preview, not the RAW file. RAW usually holds a little more highlight detail than the camera shows, often about ½ to 1 stop. Treat the camera's warning as a slightly early warning.

ProblemJPGRAW
Highlights blinking on the cameraGoneOften ½ to 1 stop recoverable
Highlights still white in Lightroom with Highlights at −100GoneGone. The RAW file itself is clipped.
1 stop too darkFixable, with some noiseFixable, nearly clean
2–3 stops too darkNoisy, with blotchy colorUsable, noticeably noisier
4 or more stops too darkNot usableVery noisy. Depends on the camera.

These are typical for recent interchangeable-lens cameras. Older cameras and phones have less room. Test your own camera: shoot the same scene at several exposures and correct each one in Lightroom.

Expose to the right

When you shoot RAW and have time, make the exposure as bright as you can without clipping the highlights that matter, then darken it in editing. On the histogram, the graph sits as far right as it can without touching the edge.

It works because more light makes a cleaner image. Noise lives mostly in the dark tones. A photo exposed brighter and darkened later has less noise in its shadows than one exposed darker and brightened later. You'll also hear that the brightest stop holds half of the file's tonal levels. That's true of how sensors record, but noise is the bigger reason.

  • RAW only. A JPG exposed to the right is just too bright, and darkening it costs quality.
  • Add light, not ISO. The extra exposure has to come from a wider aperture or a slower shutter. Raising ISO to push the graph right doesn't add light, and it uses up highlight room.
  • Expect bright previews. Photos look too bright on the camera's screen. That's the point.
  • Skip it when there's no time. For fast-moving news and sports, a normal exposure that protects the highlights is safer.

Shooting modes

The mode dial decides who sets the exposure: you, or the camera. In SJMC, shoot in manual first. Use aperture or shutter priority for specific situations. Don't use auto.

Manual

You choose all three settings, so every decision about depth of field, motion, and noise is yours. Exposure stays the same from frame to frame, which matters when you shoot a series in the same light.

Use the exposure meter in the viewfinder as a guide, not a rule. It assumes the scene is medium gray. A white bird against snow or a performer under a spotlight will fool it. Check the histogram.

Finding the right exposure in manual

In manual mode, the camera's light meter shows whether your settings match the light. It reads 0 when the camera thinks the exposure is right. Pick a scene, then change the settings until the meter reads 0.

Light meter

−3−2−10+1+2+3

Each step on a slider is one stop: double or half the light. The meter aims for medium gray, so it can be wrong for very bright or very dark scenes. See Exposure compensation and Histograms and clipping.

Aperture priority

You set the aperture and ISO. The camera picks the shutter speed. Use it when depth of field matters and the light changes faster than you can adjust, such as following someone from shade into sun at an outdoor event.

  • Watch the shutter speed. In dim light the camera will drop it too slow to hold steady or freeze motion.
  • If photos come out too bright or too dark, use exposure compensation to correct the camera.

Shutter priority

You set the shutter speed and ISO. The camera picks the aperture. Use it for action, when freezing motion matters most, or for blur on purpose.

  • Watch for a blinking aperture number. It means the lens can't open or close far enough, and the photo will be too dark or too bright.
  • Use exposure compensation the same way as in aperture priority.

Auto

The camera makes every decision, including whether to fire the flash. It aims for an average photo, not the one you want, and you can't defend choices you didn't make. Don't use it for SJMC work.

Exposure compensation

The meter aims for medium gray. When a scene is mostly bright or mostly dark, the meter's 0 gives the wrong result. Bright scenes come out too dark, and dark scenes come out too bright. Exposure compensation means setting the exposure brighter or darker than the meter says.

SceneAt the meter's 0Compensate
Snow, a sunny beach, a white bird against a bright skyGray and too dark+1 to +2
A person in front of a bright window (backlit)The person is too dark+1 to +2
A black dog, dark clothing on a dark backgroundGray and too bright−1
A performer in a spotlight on a dark stageThe face is too bright−1 to −2

These are starting points. Check the histogram and the highlight warning after the first shot.

Compensating in manual

In manual, you don't need a separate control. Set the exposure so the meter reads the amount you want. For +1, change your settings until the meter shows +1. Each stop on any of the three settings moves the meter one step. Change whichever setting you can spare: a slower shutter if nothing is moving, a wider aperture if depth of field doesn't matter, or a higher ISO as a last choice.

Compensating in aperture or shutter priority

In a priority mode the camera sets one of the three settings, so the meter always sits at 0. To go brighter or darker, use the camera's exposure compensation control. The camera then changes the setting it controls:

  • In aperture priority, it changes the shutter speed.
  • In shutter priority, it changes the aperture.

Compensation stays set until you change it, even after you turn the camera off. Set it back to 0 when the light changes, or the next scene will be too bright or too dark.

Where to find it: On most cameras, hold the +/− button and turn a dial. Many Fujifilm and Sony cameras have a dial on top marked −3 to +3. On many Canon cameras, turn the rear dial in Av or Tv mode. The amount shows on the meter in the viewfinder.

Focus

ManualMFLow light, through glass or fences, macro, or when autofocus keeps missing

Focus points

Don't let the camera pick from all its focus points. It usually picks whatever is closest or has the most contrast. Choose one point yourself and put it on what matters. For people and animals, that's the nearest eye.

Many cameras have eye detection for people, animals, and birds. Use it, and check that it found the right eye.

Focal length and lenses

Focal length is the distance, in millimeters, from the point where the lens brings light together to the sensor. You don't need to measure it. What matters is what it does: a longer focal length magnifies the subject and shows a narrower slice of the scene, and a shorter one shows more of the scene with everything smaller.

Focal lengthNameUse it for
14–24 mmUltra wideSmall rooms, architecture, big landscapes, crowds from inside
24–35 mmWideStreet, events, and environmental portraits that show where a person is
35–70 mmStandardEveryday shooting. Close to how the scene looks to your eye. A 50 mm prime is a common first lens.
70–105 mmShort telephotoHead-and-shoulders portraits, details from a few steps away
105–300 mmTelephotoSports, speakers on a stage, wildlife and birds, subjects you can't walk up to
300 mm and upSuper telephotoPro sports from the sidelines, distant birds

Prime and zoom lenses

  • Prime lenses have one fixed focal length. They're usually sharper and open wider (f/1.2, f/1.4, f/1.8), which helps in low light and gives shallower depth of field. To change the framing, you move your feet.
  • Zoom lenses cover a range, such as 24–70 mm or 70–200 mm. Some keep the same widest aperture through the range. Cheaper ones open less wide as you zoom in, so a lens marked f/3.5–5.6 lets in less light at the long end.

Focal length changes more than magnification

  • Depth of field: a longer focal length gives shallower depth of field. Three things set depth of field: the aperture, the focal length, and how close you are to the subject. A wider aperture, a longer lens, or getting closer each make it shallower.
  • Compression: a long lens makes the background look closer to the subject and larger behind it. A wide lens makes the background look farther away and smaller. With a long lens you stand farther back to fit the same subject in the frame, and that distance is what flattens the space. Faces look flatter and more natural from farther back with a short telephoto.
  • Distortion up close: a wide lens close to a face makes whatever is nearest the lens, usually the nose, look too big. Step back, or use a longer lens for portraits.
  • Camera shake: magnification also magnifies your hand movement. Keep the shutter speed at least 1 over the focal length. See Shutter speed.

Compression can change what a news photo seems to say. A long lens can make a line of people look packed together, or make a car look right on top of a pedestrian. Choose the focal length on purpose, and say in the caption if the scene was less crowded than it looks.

Crop sensors and phones

Focal lengths printed on lenses are for a full-frame sensor, the size of one frame of 35 mm film. On a full-frame camera, the number on the lens is what you get.

  • Crop sensors are smaller and show a narrower slice of the scene with the same lens. Multiply by the crop factor to compare: about 1.6 on Canon crop cameras, 1.5 on Nikon and Sony. A 50 mm lens on a Canon crop camera frames like an 80 mm lens on full frame.
  • Phones have a few separate lenses, labeled something like 0.5×, 1×, and 3×. Settings between those numbers usually crop the photo instead of using a real lens, which throws pixels away. See Cropping costs pixels. Phone specs list focal lengths as full-frame equivalents, such as about 24–26 mm for the 1× lens.

Where to find it: The focal length of every photo is saved in its metadata. In Lightroom, open the Metadata panel in Library.

White balance

White balance tells the camera what color the light is, so whites look white. Indoor bulbs are orange, shade is blue, and the camera has to correct for it.

  • In RAW, white balance can be changed later with no loss. Auto white balance is fine as a starting point.
  • In JPG, white balance is built into the file. Fixing it later costs quality. Set it before you shoot.

For a series in the same light, use one manual setting so the photos match. See the Kelvin table on Video.

Shoot RAW for anything you'll edit. Many photographers shoot RAW + JPG: the JPG for sending quickly, the RAW for the final edit. Breaking news sometimes uses JPG alone for speed.

RAW files have a different extension for each camera brand: .CR3 (Canon), .NEF (Nikon), .ARW (Sony), .RAF (Fujifilm). DNG is Adobe's open RAW format, used by some cameras and phones.

Phone photos

  • iPhones save photos as HEIC by default. It's smaller than JPG, but some apps and websites can't open it. Settings › Camera › Formats › Most Compatible saves JPG instead.
  • Newer iPhones can shoot ProRAW, a DNG file with more editing room. Turn it on in the same Formats menu. Files are large.
  • Phones combine several frames and sharpen every photo automatically. Check the details at 100% before using a phone photo in professional work.

Non-destructive editing

Lightroom and Camera Raw never change the original file. They save your edits as instructions and apply them when you export. You can always go back to the original.

Lightroom Classic keeps a catalog that points to your photos where they're stored, like a video project points to its media. If you move or rename the folders outside Lightroom, it marks the photos as missing. Move folders inside Lightroom instead.

Size and cropping

Megapixels and print size

Megapixels are millions of pixels: width × height. At 300 PPI:

CameraPixelsLargest print at 300 PPI
12 MP (many phones)4032 × 302413.4 × 10 in
24 MP6000 × 400020 × 13.3 in
45 MP8192 × 546427.3 × 18.2 in

Larger prints work at lower PPI because people see them from farther away. See Viewing distance on Graphics.

Cropping costs pixels

Cropping throws pixels away. Crop half the width and half the height, and a 24 MP photo becomes 6 MP. Get close when you shoot, with your feet or a longer lens, instead of planning to crop.

Some publications limit cropping on news photos. Follow your outlet's rules.

Metadata

Every photo file carries information besides the picture.

  • EXIF is written by the camera: date, time, camera, lens, settings, and often GPS location.
  • IPTC is written by you: caption, photographer's name, copyright, keywords. News organizations read it to credit and caption photos.

Location data

Phones and some cameras save the exact GPS location in each photo. Anyone with the file can see where it was taken. Remove it before sharing a photo that could reveal a source's home, a private person's location, or your own.

Where to find it: In Lightroom Classic's Export window, under Metadata, turn on Remove Location Info. To stop an iPhone saving location, Settings › Privacy & Security › Location Services › Camera, set to Never.

Adding a caption and credit

Write the caption and your name into the file before you send it, so they travel with the photo.

Where to find it: In Lightroom Classic, the Metadata panel in the Library module. In Photoshop, File › File Info. Lightroom can add your name and copyright to every photo on import with a metadata preset.

Editing standards

Photojournalism

A news photo has to show what the camera saw. Editing can make it look like the scene did. It can't change what's in it.

AllowedNot allowed
Exposure, contrast, and white balance correctionAdding, removing, or moving anything in the frame
Light dodging and burning, as in a darkroomCloning or healing out people, signs, wires, or objects
Removing dust spots from the sensorCombining photos
Cropping that doesn't change the meaningGenerative fill, AI expansion, or AI upscaling
Converting to black and whiteColor or tone changes that hide or change facts
Noise reduction and sharpening that only clean up the photoSharpening or AI that makes an unreadable face, sign, or plate readable

Staging or directing a news photo is also not allowed. Don't ask people to redo a moment for the camera. Portraits are different: posing is expected, and the caption shouldn't suggest otherwise.

Strategic communication

Retouching is normal in advertising and brand work: removing blemishes, cleaning backgrounds, compositing product shots. The limits:

  • Don't change what a product does or how it looks in use. A retouched result in a beauty or fitness ad can be false advertising.
  • Follow the client's rules and any platform rules on disclosing edited or AI-made images. See AI: Disclosure and labels.
  • Keep the unedited original.

Noise reduction and sharpening

Both tools change fine detail. Noise reduction smooths it. Sharpening adds contrast along edges so they look crisper. Used lightly, both make a photo look more like the scene did. Pushed too far, they change what the photo shows.

Do them last, after exposure, white balance, and tone.

Start in the camera

  • Neither tool fixes blur. Sharpening a photo with motion blur or missed focus makes the blur look gritty, not sharp. Use a fast enough shutter speed and check focus.
  • Noise reduction always costs detail. Use the lowest ISO the shot allows, and don't underexpose. A dark photo brightened in editing is noisier. See ISO and Expose to the right.
  • Start from a sharp aperture. Most lenses are sharpest from about f/4 to f/11. Wide open they are softer, and at f/16–f/22 everything softens a little.

Noise reduction

Kind of noiseWhat it looks likeWhat reducing it does
LuminanceSpeckles of light and dark, like grainSmooths the speckles. Too much makes skin look plastic and erases texture in hair, fabric, and grass.
ColorRed, green, and blue blotches, mostly in shadowsRemoves the blotches. Lightroom already applies some to every RAW file, so it rarely needs more.

AI noise reduction (Denoise in Lightroom and Camera Raw) cleans noise much better than the sliders, and works on RAW files only. It predicts what clean detail should look like, so at high settings it can invent texture that wasn't there. Check faces, text, and edges at 100% after using it.

  • Reduce noise only until it stops distracting at the size people will see the photo. Some grain is fine. Smeared detail looks worse than grain.
  • Judge at 100% zoom, then at the size the photo will be shown.
  • Don't use noise reduction to smooth skin. That's retouching. See Editing standards.

Sharpening

Sharpening raises the contrast where light and dark areas meet. It can't add detail the camera didn't record.

Lightroom sliderWhat it does
AmountHow strong the sharpening is. Lightroom starts RAW files at 40.
RadiusHow wide an area along each edge gets sharpened. Keep it near 1.0 for most photos.
DetailHow much fine texture gets sharpened. Higher brings out texture, and noise with it.
MaskingLimits sharpening to strong edges. Raise it to keep sharpening off skin and sky. Hold Option (Alt in Windows) while you drag it: white areas get sharpened, black areas don't.

Signs of oversharpening:

  • Bright or dark outlines (halos) along edges, especially against the sky
  • Noise turned into gritty speckles
  • Skin, hair, and fabric that look wiry or rough
  • Jagged diagonal lines

Best practices:

  • Sharpen twice, lightly. Once while editing, to make up for the softness every camera sensor adds. Again when you export, for the size and medium. Shrinking a photo softens it a little, and export sharpening makes up for it. See Exporting.
  • Phone photos and camera JPGs are already sharpened. Add little or none.
  • Portraits need less sharpening than buildings or landscapes. Use Masking so skin stays smooth while eyes and hair stay crisp.
  • Texture, Clarity, and Dehaze also change detail. Use them gently. Large Clarity values make a photo look gritty and overworked.

In news photos

Normal noise reduction and sharpening are corrections, like exposure and white balance. They're allowed as long as they only make the photo look like the scene did.

  • Don't make the unreadable readable. Never use sharpening or AI tools to make a blurry face, license plate, sign, or document readable. The new detail is a guess, and publishing a guess as fact is fabrication. If it can't be read in the original, it isn't in the photo.
  • Don't remove or add atmosphere. Dehaze can erase smoke, fog, or haze, and negative Dehaze adds it. Smoke from a fire or haze over a city can be the news. Leave it as it was.
  • Don't exaggerate. Heavy Clarity or sharpening can make a scene look grittier or more dramatic than it was, or make a person's skin look harsher than in life. That changes how readers judge the moment and the person.
  • Ask before using AI noise reduction. Outlets' rules differ. AI upscaling is not allowed (see Editing standards).
  • Treat a set alike. Edit photos of the same event the same way, so no one looks better or worse because of the edit.
  • Keep the original. Editors may ask for the RAW or unedited file.

In strategic communication

Product and brand work allows more: heavy noise reduction on a product shot, sharpening details like stitching or texture, smoothing a backdrop. The limit is the same as for other retouching: don't make a product look different from what buyers will get.

Where to find it: In Lightroom Classic, the Develop module's Detail panel has Sharpening, Noise Reduction, and the Denoise button. Camera Raw has the same Detail panel. In Photoshop, Filter › Sharpen › Smart Sharpen. To view at 100%, press Z in Lightroom or Command + 1 (Ctrl + 1 in Windows) in Photoshop.

Captions and alt text

Captions

A news caption is usually two sentences.

  1. The first sentence says who, what, where, and when, in present tense.
  2. The second gives background, in past tense.

Maria Lopez of Iowa City fills a bird feeder in her backyard on Saturday, Feb. 14, 2026. Lopez has counted birds for the Great Backyard Bird Count for 12 years.

Check every name's spelling with the person. Identify people left to right when there's more than one.

Alt text

Alt text describes the photo for people who can't see it. A caption adds context everyone can see. They do different jobs, so a published photo usually needs both. See Accessibility on Web.

Quick answers

Why are my photos blurry?
The shutter speed is too slow for your hands or the subject, or focus landed on the wrong spot. See Shutter speed and Focus.
Why are my photos grainy?
The ISO is high, maybe because auto ISO raised it. See ISO. To clean up photos you already have, see Noise reduction.
Why do photos of the same scene come out different brightnesses?
Auto ISO or a priority mode is adjusting each frame. Shoot in manual with a fixed ISO. See Shooting modes.
Why is the sky white with no detail?
The highlights are clipped. Check the histogram and expose for the bright areas. In RAW you may be able to recover some of it. See Histograms and clipping.
Why won't my RAW files open?
RAW needs Lightroom, Camera Raw, or another RAW editor, sometimes a recent version for a new camera. See Camera files.
Why won't my iPhone photo open or upload?
It's HEIC. Export it as JPG, or set the phone to Most Compatible. See Phone photos.
Why does the background look so close behind my subject?
That's compression from a long focal length and standing far back. For more space between them, use a wider lens and move closer. See Focal length and lenses.
Can I sharpen a blurry photo?
Not really. Sharpening makes edges look crisper but can't fix motion blur or missed focus. See Noise reduction and sharpening.
Can I use AI to make a face or license plate readable in a news photo?
No. The added detail is a guess. See Noise reduction and sharpening in news photos.
Can I remove a distracting sign from a news photo?
No. Reframe when you shoot. See Editing standards.
Can I use a photo I found online?
Only with a license or permission. See Rights and Credit.
