Rights and Credit

When you can use other people's photos, graphics, music, video, fonts, and writing, and how to credit them.

This page explains US law in plain terms. It isn't legal advice. For a specific case, ask your instructor or the University of Iowa Libraries.

Copyright

Copyright protects creative work: photos, illustrations, video, music, recordings, writing, software, and fonts. It gives the creator the right to decide who can copy, change, share, and sell the work.

  • It starts the moment the work is made. No © symbol, registration, or notice is needed.
  • It lasts a long time: the creator's life plus 70 years. For work made by a company, 95 years from publication.
  • Finding a work online doesn't give you any rights to it. Almost everything online is copyrighted by someone.

What copyright doesn't cover

  • Facts and data. A game score, a bird count, or a population number can't be copyrighted. A chart, table, or article someone made from those facts can.
  • Ideas. The idea of a poster for a baseball game is free to use. A specific poster design isn't.
  • Names, titles, and short phrases. These may be protected by trademark instead (see People and brands).

What you can use

You can use someone else's work if one of these is true:

SourceWhat it means
Public domainNo one owns it. Use it for anything.
Creative CommonsThe owner allows reuse under stated conditions. Follow them.
Stock licenseYou or the school paid for a license, or a library offers it under its own terms.
PermissionThe owner said yes, in writing.
Fair useThe law allows some uses without permission. Decided case by case.

If none of these is true, don't use it. Make your own, or find something else.

Public domain

Public domain work belongs to everyone. You can copy it, change it, and sell what you make. Crediting it is still good practice.

In the US, these are in the public domain:

  • Work published before 1931. Each January 1, another year's work joins.
  • Work made by US federal government employees as part of their jobs, such as NASA images and many US Fish and Wildlife Service photos. This doesn't include state or city governments.
  • Work the owner has released with a CC0 or public domain mark.

A new photo or recording of an old work can have its own copyright. Beethoven's music is public domain. A 2024 orchestra recording of it isn't. Check both the work and the version you found.

Where to find it: The Library of Congress, the National Archives, Smithsonian Open Access, and Wikimedia Commons all mark public domain items. Check the label on each item, since these sites also hold copyrighted work.

Creative Commons

Creative Commons (CC) licenses let creators allow reuse without being asked each time. Each license is built from four parts:

PartNameWhat you must do
BYAttributionCredit the creator. Every CC license includes this.
SAShareAlikeRelease what you make under the same license.
NCNonCommercialDon't use it to sell or promote anything. Client work and ads count as commercial.
NDNoDerivativesUse it unchanged. No edits, crops into a new design, or remixes.

The license name lists its parts. CC BY-NC means credit the creator and don't use it commercially. CC BY is the most open: credit the creator and do anything else.

A news story on a news organization's website is usually treated as commercial, because the site sells ads. Avoid NC material in published journalism unless the outlet says it's fine.

Where to find it: Openverse (openverse.org) searches CC images and audio. Flickr, Wikimedia Commons, and Free Music Archive let you filter by license.

Stock and royalty-free

Stock sites sell licenses to photos, video, music, illustrations, and sound effects.

  • Royalty-free means you pay once and don't pay again for each use. It doesn't mean free.
  • Rights-managed means the price depends on the use: where, how big, how long. You pay again for a new use.
  • Editorial use only means news and commentary only. No ads, promotions, or anything that sells a product. Photos of real people, brands, and events are often marked this way.

Read the license for each item. Free stock sites such as Unsplash and Pexels have their own licenses with limits, for example on selling the photo unchanged or implying a person in a photo endorses something.

A license usually covers you, for one project. Keep a copy of the license or receipt with the project files.

Fair use

Fair use lets you use copyrighted work without permission for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, and research. There's no list of uses that are always fair. A court weighs four factors.

The four factors

  1. Purpose of your use. Commenting on, criticizing, or reporting on the work leans fair. Using it for the same purpose as the original, or to sell something, leans against. Strategic communication work for a client is commercial, which makes fair use much harder to claim.
  2. Kind of work. Using factual work (a news photo of a public event, a government report) leans fair. Using highly creative work (a song, a film, an illustration) leans against.
  3. How much you used. Use only what you need for your point. Using the most important part, such as the chorus of a song or the key frame of a video, leans against even if it's short.
  4. Effect on the owner's market. If your use could replace a sale or license of the original, it leans against. This factor often decides the case.

Changing the work isn't enough

Adding a filter, cropping, or remixing doesn't make a use fair by itself. In 2023 the Supreme Court ruled against the Andy Warhol Foundation for licensing a Warhol print based on Lynn Goldsmith's photo of Prince. The print looked different, but it was used for the same purpose as the photo: a magazine illustration of Prince.

Examples

UseLikely fair?
A film review that shows a 10-second clip and discusses itLikely fair
A news story about a viral post that shows a screenshot of the postLikely fair
A news story about a museum that uses the museum's photo of a painting it isn't discussingUnclear. Ask the museum.
A news story that uses a photographer's image as decoration, with creditLikely not fair
A class video with a popular song playing under it, posted publiclyLikely not fair
A client's social ad that uses a movie clipNot fair

Fair use in class and in your portfolio

Turning in work to an instructor is a teaching use, and gives you more room. Once that work goes online, into a portfolio, or into a publication, it's judged like any other published work. Plan for publication from the start, so you don't have to rebuild a project later.

Getting permission

If you want to use something and none of the other options apply, ask the owner. Many photographers and musicians say yes to students, sometimes for free.

Get the answer in writing. An email is enough. Say:

  • exactly which work you want to use
  • where it will appear (class project, portfolio site, publication, client's social media)
  • for how long
  • how you'll credit them

Keep the reply with your project files. If the work is used later somewhere else, ask again. Permission for a class project doesn't cover a client's ad.

The person who posted something isn't always the owner. A meme account didn't take the photo it shared. Find the original creator.

Rules by medium

Photos and graphics

  • News photos from AP, Reuters, and Getty are licensed to news outlets that pay for them. You can't use them unless your outlet has a subscription.
  • Handout photos that an organization sends to the press can be used for news about that organization. Credit them as "Photo courtesy of [organization]."
  • Client logos and brand photos: get them from the client, and get it in writing that the client has the rights to them.
  • Screenshots of a website, app, or game are copies. Use them when you're reporting on or reviewing that thing.

Music

Every recorded song has two copyrights:

  • the composition (the melody and lyrics), usually owned by the songwriter and a music publisher
  • the recording (the specific performance), usually owned by a record label

To put a song in a video, you need permission for both. This is called a sync license. It's expensive and slow, so most video work uses licensed music libraries instead.

  • Production music libraries license tracks for video and podcasts. Some are free with credit. Some charge per track or by subscription.
  • App sound libraries (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube Shorts) are licensed for use inside that app only. A video made with a library song and then posted on a website or another platform isn't covered. Business and brand accounts usually get a smaller, commercial-only library.
  • Playing a song on your own instrument still uses the composition. You need permission for it unless it's in the public domain.

There is no "under 30 seconds" or "under 10 seconds" rule. A few seconds of a song can still infringe.

Sound effects

Sound effects are recordings and have copyright. Use a library that licenses them, record your own, or use CC0 sounds. Read whether the license allows commercial use.

Video

  • Clips from films, TV, news broadcasts, and YouTube are copyrighted. Short clips can be fair use when you're reporting on or reviewing the video itself.
  • Stock footage needs a license, like stock photos.
  • Downloading a video from YouTube or a social platform usually breaks that platform's terms of service, even when your use would be fair use.
  • Music inside a clip has its own rights. A clip you're allowed to use may still contain a song you aren't.

Fonts

Fonts are software. Each comes with a license that says where you can use it: on your computer, on a website, in an app, or in a logo. See Font files and licenses on the Type page.

Writing

  • Quoting a short passage with credit is normal in journalism and usually fair use. Reprinting a whole article, poem, or song lyric isn't.
  • Press releases are written to be reused. Journalists still rewrite them and attribute any facts or quotes.
  • Copying words or ideas without credit is plagiarism, even when it's legal. Plagiarism is an ethics problem. Copyright is a legal one. You can break either rule without breaking the other.

Social media posts

  • The person who made a post owns it, not the platform. The platform's terms give the platform rights, not you.
  • Embedding a post with the platform's embed code shows it from the original source. News outlets usually do this. Courts haven't fully settled whether embedding can infringe, but it's the common practice.
  • Downloading and re-uploading a post's photo or video is copying. Ask the creator first. Newsrooms ask even during breaking news.

People and brands

Photos and video of people

  • News use: in the US, you can photograph and record people in public places and publish it as news without their permission.
  • Commercial use: using a person's face, name, or voice to promote or sell something needs their signed permission, called a model release. This includes ads, brand social media, and promotional videos.
  • Minors: a parent or guardian signs the release.
  • Private property: a recognizable building, artwork, or interior used in an ad may need a property release from the owner.

Keep signed releases with the project files.

Trademarks

Trademarks are names, logos, and slogans that identify a company or product. Trademark law is about not confusing people over who made something or who approves it.

  • In journalism, you can name and show a brand or logo when you report on it.
  • In strategic communication, don't use another company's logo or name in a way that suggests they made, sponsor, or approve your work.
  • Don't change a company's logo, even its color. Use the version from its press or brand page.
  • University of Iowa marks (the Tigerhawk, the wordmark) have their own rules. Check with the university before using them.

Your own work

  • You own the copyright to work you make on your own, including most class work.
  • Work you make as an employee, as part of your job, belongs to the employer. This includes internships and student jobs.
  • Work you make as a freelancer belongs to you unless a written contract transfers it. Clients usually ask for it in the contract.
  • Get it in writing whether you can show client or employer work in your portfolio. Many contracts allow it. Some don't, or require waiting until the work is public.

Credit lines

A credit line tells the audience who made something. Credit is required for CC work, often required by stock and permission agreements, and expected in journalism.

Credit doesn't replace permission. A credit line on work you had no right to use is still infringement.

What to include

For Creative Commons work, include the title, author, source, and license, with links when the work is online. For other work, give the creator and the source, plus "used with permission" or the license name when the agreement asks for it.

Where the credit goes

  • Print and web: beside or below the image, in smaller type. Some publications group credits at the end.
  • Video: on screen when the material appears, in the end credits, or both. List music in the end credits.
  • Podcasts: in the show notes. Read music credits on air if the license asks.
  • Social media: in the post caption, or tag the creator.
  • Ads: usually no visible credit. The license is what matters. Keep it on file.

What doesn't give you permission

None of these make it OK to use someone else's work:

  • Giving credit.
  • "It's just for a class," once the work is posted or published.
  • It's for a nonprofit or you aren't making money.
  • You used less than 10 seconds, 30 seconds, or 10% of it.
  • You changed it by some percentage.
  • It was on Google, Pinterest, or social media.
  • It had no © symbol.
  • You bought a copy. Owning a song, movie, or print doesn't give you the right to reuse it.
  • Someone else used it without trouble.

Quick answers

Can I use this image I found online?
Only if it's public domain, under a license you follow, covered by fair use, or you have written permission. See What you can use.
Can I use a popular song in my video?
Almost never without a sync license. Use a music library instead. See Music.
If I give credit, is it OK?
No. Credit is required, but it doesn't give you permission. See Credit lines.
Can I use a TikTok sound in a video on my portfolio site?
No. App sound libraries are licensed for use inside that app only. See Music.
Do I need a release to photograph someone?
Not for news in a public place. Yes for ads and anything promotional. See People and brands.
Can I use a company's logo?
Yes, when you're reporting on that company. Not in a way that suggests the company made or approves your work. See Trademarks.
Can I put class work that uses someone else's material in my portfolio?
Only if you'd be allowed to publish it anywhere else. See Fair use in class and in your portfolio.
Who owns work I made for a client?
You do, unless a contract transfers it or you were an employee. Get it in writing. See Your own work.
