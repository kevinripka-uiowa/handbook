Logos

How professionals build a logo, set up its colors, and hand off the files a client, printer, or web developer needs.

Anyone can make something that looks like a logo in a few minutes. A professional logo is different in three ways: the client owns it, it works everywhere it shows up, and it comes with the right files for each job. Most of what goes wrong with student and small-business logos is in the second and third parts.

A logo is never one file. It shows up on a sign, a menu, a T-shirt, a website, a social media profile, and a newspaper ad. Each place has a different shape and different color limits.

Logos, AI, and Canva

AI image tools and Canva can make a logo-shaped picture fast. What they don't give you is a logo you own, built as vector artwork, with set colors and the files a client needs.

What goes wrong

  • You may not own it. A logo made by AI can't be copyrighted. Canva's templates and stock graphics are licensed to everyone, and Canva's license doesn't allow them in a trademark. See AI: Copyright and ownership.
  • It isn't vector. AI image tools make pixels. Auto-tracing them gives lumpy curves and hundreds of extra anchor points. A logo has to work on a business card and a banner. See Graphics: Raster and vector.
  • It's one version. You get one picture, usually a horizontal lockup in full color. There's no icon, no one-color version, and no reversed version.
  • The colors aren't set. Canva and AI tools work in RGB. They can't make a spot color, and you can't control how the color turns into CMYK. The logo prints a different color at every shop.
  • It looks like everyone else's. AI and templates land on the most common answer. A logo is supposed to set a business apart.

How to use AI on a logo

The SJMC approach names the kind of AI use, not the feature. See AI: Three kinds of AI use. For logos:

UseOn a logoOK?
Grunt workRemoving a background from a reference photo, finding fonts that look like a sample, checking spelling in the nameYes. Check the result.
DirectionRecoloring artwork you drew to swatches you picked, exporting every file at onceYes. You make the choices.
CreationGenerating marks or whole logosOnly for mood boards, after your own sketches. The final mark is drawn by a person.

Ideas start with you, on paper. Once you have your own sketches, you can talk them through with AI to push them further. The test: you can explain every choice in the logo without AI.

Your class sets its own rules. Some logo projects allow no AI at all.

Where Canva fits

Canva is fine for using a finished logo: placing the client's PNG on a social post or a flyer. It isn't a tool for building the logo. Build it in a vector app such as Illustrator, then bring the exported files into Canva.

How a logo gets built

  1. Research. Who the client is, who the audience is, and where the logo will show up. List the places: sign, cups, website, social profile, shirts.
  2. Sketch on paper. Dozens of small thumbnails, fast. The first ideas are the ones everyone has. Keep going past them.
  3. Pick and refine. Choose a few and sketch them larger. Get feedback.
  4. Build it in vector. In Illustrator, from simple shapes and as few anchor points as you can. Check it in Outline view (View › Outline) for stray points and overlaps.
  5. Set the name. Pick a typeface that fits the idea and the mark. Kern and track by eye. Type is covered on Type.
  6. Make the layouts and color versions. See below.
  7. Test it. Small (32 px for the icon), in one color, in black, and reversed on a dark photo. Fix what breaks.
  8. Set up the colors and export the package.

Layouts

Most logos come in three layouts. Each uses the same mark and the same type. Only the arrangement changes.

LayoutWhat it isWhere it goes
HorizontalThe mark and the name side by sideSigns, website headers, letterhead, email signatures
Vertical (stacked)The mark above the nameCups, menu covers, posters, T-shirt fronts
IconThe mark alone, on a squareSocial media profiles, app icons, browser tabs, stamps, stickers

Social media apps crop profile pictures to a circle, so keep the icon's mark inside the circle. Check that the icon still reads at 32 px. If its details disappear, simplify the mark.

Color versions

Each layout comes in four color versions. Brand guides also call them color variations or logo versions.

Color versionWhat it isWhere it goes
Full colorThe brand colors, usually one or twoMost print and screen work
One colorEverything in one brand colorScreen-printed shirts, embroidery, stamped cups and bags, promotional pens. One ink is the cheapest way to print.
BlackEverything in 100% blackNewspaper ads, forms, receipts, office printers, sponsor lists
ReversedWhite, made for dark backgroundsDark photos, dark mode, black shirts, dark signs

A reversed file is white artwork on a transparent background. Don't save a black box behind it. On a red shirt, the box prints too.

Keep brand colors to two or fewer. Every spot color is another ink, and every ink costs money on shirts, packaging, and printed stationery.

Logo color: spot, CMYK, and RGB

A brand color is written three ways, one for each way it gets made. A brand guide lists all three. RGB, CMYK, and gamut are explained on Graphics: Color.

RecipeExample (a cardinal red)Used for
Spot (Pantone)PANTONE 186 CPrinting with one premixed ink: shirts, packaging, business cards, signs
Process (CMYK)0, 100, 81, 4Printing with four inks: digital printers, magazines, flyers
Screen (hex)#C8102EWebsites, social media, slides, video

Spot color

A spot color is one ink, mixed before printing and printed solid. It's the same color on every job, with every printer. The Pantone Matching System (PMS) names the inks. The letter after the number is the paper: C for coated (glossy), U for uncoated. The same ink looks darker and duller on uncoated paper.

Spot color is also how you get colors CMYK can't make: bright orange, neon, and metallic gold or silver.

Why you type in each recipe

  • Converting a spot color to CMYK can shift it. Some spot colors can't be made in CMYK at all.
  • Converting CMYK to RGB gives odd numbers. 100% black becomes #231F20, a dark gray, not #000000.
  • So brand guides choose each recipe by eye, and you type each one in yourself.

Black in a logo

For print, a black logo is 100% black ink only (0, 0, 0, 100), not rich black. For screens, it's #000000.

Making a spot swatch without the Pantone library

Adobe apps no longer include the Pantone color books for free. They need a paid Pantone Connect license. The SJMC labs don't have it, so you build each spot swatch yourself, with the Pantone name and your own CMYK values. Printers match the ink by its name, so a swatch you build works the same as one from the library.

1. Pick your colors, then find the Pantone match

  1. Pick each brand color by eye first. Write down its CMYK values and its hex. Illustrator's Color Picker shows both.
  2. Find the closest Pantone color in the Pantone Color Finder. Compare its swatches with your color.
  3. Write down the Pantone color's exact name, such as PANTONE 186 C.

The Color Finder shows Pantone names and swatches, but not CMYK or hex values. Your CMYK and hex come from the colors you picked. Screens show ink colors only roughly, so the match is by eye.

2. Build the swatch

  1. Open the Swatches panel and choose New Swatch.
  2. Swatch Name: the Pantone name, exactly as Pantone writes it: PANTONE 186 C. No nicknames like "brand red."
  3. Color Type: Spot Color.
  4. Global: on (Illustrator).
  5. Color Mode: CMYK. Type the CMYK values you wrote down. They control how the color looks on screen and in a CMYK proof.

Where to find it: Illustrator: Window › Swatches, then the New Swatch button at the bottom of the panel. A spot swatch shows a small dot in its corner; a global swatch shows a white triangle. InDesign: Window › Color › Swatches, then New Color Swatch from the panel menu, with Color Type set to Spot. To check the inks, Illustrator has Window › Separations Preview: your Pantone ink should appear as its own plate.

3. Make the CMYK and screen versions

  • CMYK swatch: make a second swatch with the same CMYK values and Color Type set to Process Color. Name it something like "186 C as CMYK."
  • Screen swatch: in an RGB copy of your file (File › Document Color Mode › RGB Color), edit each swatch to the hex value you wrote down.

Build with global swatches from the start. Then changing a swatch changes every shape that uses it, on every artboard. To swap colors on a copy of an artboard, select a shape and use Select › Same › Fill Color, then click the new swatch, or use Edit › Edit Colors › Recolor Artwork.

File types

FormatRaster or vectorTransparentGive it to
AIVectorYesAnother designer. The editable source file, with all text outlined.
PDFVectorYesPrinters, sign shops, and merch vendors. Keeps spot colors and CMYK.
SVGVectorYesWeb developers. Sharp at any size on a website.
PNGRasterYesEveryone else: slides, documents, email signatures, Canva, social media. Export large (2000 px wide is a good default).
JPGRasterNoOnly for places that reject PNG. It always has a background.
EPSVectorYesRarely needed now. Only if a vendor asks for it.

Print files stay CMYK or spot. Screen files are RGB. Rasters are for screens only; printers get vector files.

Outline all text in the files you hand off (Type › Create Outlines), so the logo looks right on a computer without your font. Keep a working copy with live type so you can still edit the name.

The logo package

The package is every layout in every color version, in the formats each person needs, in one zipped folder.

client-name-logo.zip
    client-name-brand-sheet.pdf
    source/     the Illustrator masters (print in CMYK, screen in RGB)
    print/      PDFs: each layout in spot, CMYK, one color, black, and reversed
    digital/    SVGs and PNGs: each layout in full color, black, and reversed

Your class sheet may list exact files. Follow it.

Naming

Name every file by one pattern: client name, layout, color version, color recipe. All lowercase, with hyphens. A client should know what's in a file without opening it.

audubon-kitchen-horizontal-fullcolor-pms.pdf
audubon-kitchen-icon-reversed-rgb.png

File naming in general is on Files: Naming files.

Let the software do the exporting

Make one artboard for each file, named what the file will be called. Then one export writes every file.

Where to find it: Illustrator: Window › Artboards to add and name artboards. File › Export › Export for Screens, Artboards tab: select all, add formats (SVG, PNG at a width of 2000 px, PDF), and set a prefix such as client-name-. The gear icon sets PNG background to Transparent. Window › Asset Export exports a single piece of artwork, such as one more icon size.

The brand sheet

A one-page PDF that goes in the package, written for a client who isn't a designer:

  • Each brand color with its Pantone name, CMYK values, and hex
  • Which file to use for which job: the print folder for printers and vendors, the digital folder for websites and social media
  • The minimum size: the smallest width where the logo still reads

Larger brands have a full brand guide with clear space, typefaces, and misuse examples. The one-page sheet is the part every client needs.

Handing it off

  1. Check the colors: print master in CMYK, screen master in RGB, spot inks named exactly.
  2. Check that all text is outlined.
  3. Open a few PNGs on a dark background to check that they're transparent.
  4. Check every file name against the pattern.
  5. Zip the folder and send a link. See Files: Zipping and packaging.
  6. Keep your working file and the package. Clients lose files and ask again.

Quick answers

Can I make a logo in Canva?
You can make a picture of one. You can't make spot colors or a vector master in Canva, and its stock graphics can't be used in a trademark. Build the logo in a vector app and use Canva to place the finished files. See Logos, AI, and Canva.
Can I make a logo with AI?
Use it for mood boards after your own sketches. The final mark should be designed by a person, so the client can own it. See AI: Copyright and ownership.
The lab doesn't have Pantone colors. How do I make a spot color?
Find the closest Pantone name in the Pantone Color Finder, then build the swatch yourself with that name, your own CMYK values, and Color Type set to Spot Color. See Making a spot swatch.
Why is my black logo dark gray on the website?
CMYK black converted to #231F20. Set black to #000000 in the RGB file. See Logo color.
Why does my reversed logo have a black box?
The background was saved into the file. Delete it and export a PNG with a transparent background. See Color versions.
Do I need EPS files?
Rarely. PDF has replaced EPS for printers. Make one only if a vendor asks.
The printer asked for a "vector logo." What do I send?
The PDF from the print folder, in the color version the job needs. See File types.
