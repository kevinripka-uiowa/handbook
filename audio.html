Audio Technical Basics

How sound is recorded, measured, edited, and delivered: microphones, levels, file formats, loudness, and exporting for podcasts, video, and social media.

People will watch a video with poor picture. They stop listening to poor sound. Most audio problems can't be fixed in editing, so most of this page is about recording.

Music and sound effects you didn't make are covered on Rights and Credit.

Microphones

Types of microphones

TypeWhat it isUse it for
DynamicSturdy and less sensitive. Picks up the voice close to it and less of the room.Podcasts in ordinary rooms, interviews in noisy places, handheld reporting
CondenserMore sensitive and detailed. Picks up more of the room. Needs power, called phantom power (48V), from the recorder or interface.Quiet, treated rooms: studios, voiceover booths
Lavalier (lav)A small clip-on mic, wired or wireless.On-camera interviews, anyone who moves while talking
ShotgunA long mic that picks up mostly what it points at.On a camera or on a boom pole above the subject, just out of frame
Built-inThe mic in a phone, camera, or laptop. It picks up the whole room.Reference sound for syncing. Not for anything you publish.

USB mics are dynamic or condenser mics that plug straight into a computer. They're fine for a solo podcast at a desk.

Pickup patterns

A mic's pickup pattern is the direction it hears from.

  • Cardioid: hears the front, rejects the back. Most podcast and handheld mics. Point the front at the mouth.
  • Supercardioid and shotgun: narrower. Aim matters more: off-axis voices sound thin and far away.
  • Omnidirectional: hears all directions equally. Most lavs. Placement matters more than aim.

Mic placement

  • Closer is better. Each time you halve the distance, the voice gets about 6 dB louder compared with the room. Moving the mic closer does more than any setting or plugin.
  • Podcast or handheld mic: about a fist's width (4–6 in) from the mouth, slightly off to the side so breaths and p-sounds don't hit it directly. Use a foam cover or pop filter.
  • Lav: on the chest, about a hand's width below the chin, on the side the person will face. Keep it away from jewelry, collars, and fabric that rubs.
  • Shotgun on a boom: above the subject, pointed down at the mouth, as close as the frame allows. A shotgun on top of a camera is usually too far away for interviews.

Recording

The room

  • Pick a soft room. Carpet, curtains, couches, and closets full of clothes soak up echo. Bare walls, glass, and tile make it.
  • Turn off what hums. Fans, heating and air conditioning, refrigerators, buzzing lights. Put a reminder on the fridge to plug it back in.
  • Silence phones. Airplane mode, for everyone in the room.
  • Listen first. Stand silently for 10 seconds before you record. Your brain tunes out noise the mic will pick up.

Room tone

Record 30 seconds of the room with everyone silent, at every location. Editors use it to fill gaps and cover edits so the background doesn't drop out. It's also the noise sample for noise reduction.

Wear headphones

Listen through headphones the whole time you record, not only at the start. The level meter can look fine while a cable crackles, a lav rubs, or the mic isn't on at all. Closed-back headphones block outside sound so you hear only the mic.

Recording on a phone

  • Get the phone close. Within about a foot of the speaker's mouth, pointed at them, mic end toward the mouth. A phone across a table records the room, not the voice.
  • Use an external mic when you can. A lav or a small wireless mic made for phones is inexpensive and is the biggest improvement.
  • Record at full quality. On an iPhone, Voice Memos saves compressed files by default. Change it to Lossless.
  • Turn on airplane mode. A call or notification can stop the recording or add a buzz.
  • Don't hold it in your hand while recording. Handling noise is loud. Set it on something soft or use a small stand.

Where to find it: On an iPhone, Settings › Apps › Voice Memos › Audio Quality › Lossless.

Backups

For an interview you can't repeat, record on two devices. A second phone or a recorder's backup track has saved many interviews. Copy files off the card or phone the same day, to two places.

Levels and clipping

Decibels on a meter

Digital audio meters measure in dBFS (decibels full scale). The top of the scale is 0 dBFS, the loudest the file can hold. Every other level is a negative number: −6, −12, −20. Closer to 0 is louder.

Where to set levels

SoundPeaks on the meter
Normal speechAround −12 dBFS
Loudest moments (laughing, shouting)No higher than −6 dBFS
Too quietBelow about −24 dBFS most of the time

Set the level while the person talks normally, then watch it. People get louder when they laugh or get excited. Leave room for it.

Set the level with the gain on the recorder or interface, not the volume in editing. Gain changes what's recorded. Turning a quiet recording up later raises the noise with it.

Clipping

Sound louder than 0 dBFS can't be recorded. The tops of the waveform are cut off flat, which is called clipping. It sounds harsh and crackly.

Clipping works like blown highlights in a photo: the information isn't there. Repair tools can reduce it a little, but a badly clipped recording can't be fixed. A recording that's a little quiet can. When in doubt, record lower.

Where to find it: In Audition, Effects › Diagnostics › DeClipper. In Audacity, Effect › Noise Removal and Repair › Clip Fix. Use either as a last resort.

32-bit float recorders

Some newer recorders and wireless mics record in 32-bit float. These files hold sound far above 0 dBFS, so a level set too high can be turned down later without clipping. It doesn't fix a mic that's too far away, a noisy room, or a loud sound distorting the mic itself. Set levels as if it didn't exist.

Sample rate, bit depth, and channels

Sample rate

Digital audio measures the sound wave thousands of times per second. The sample rate is how many times.

Sample rateUse it for
48 kHzAnything that goes with video. Also fine for podcasts.
44.1 kHzMusic, and podcasts if that's what your editor is set to
96 kHzMusic recording and sound design that will be slowed or pitched down. Not needed for voice.

Pick one rate and record everything at it. Mixing 44.1 and 48 kHz files can cause pitch changes or drifting sync. Set the recorder to 48 kHz unless you have a reason not to.

Bit depth

Bit depth is how finely each measurement records loudness. It sets how much room there is between the quietest and loudest sound.

  • 24-bit: record at this. It leaves room to record lower without adding noise.
  • 16-bit: fine for finished files. CDs are 16-bit.
  • 32-bit float: see 32-bit float recorders.

Mono and stereo

  • Mono is one channel. One mic makes one mono recording. Voice is usually mono.
  • Stereo is two channels, left and right. Music and ambient sound are usually stereo.

Many recorders and cameras save each mic to its own channel of a stereo file: a lav on the left, a camera mic on the right. If you edit that file as-is, the voice plays in only one ear. In editing, set the clip so the voice channel plays in both ears.

Where to find it: In Premiere Pro, select the clip and use Clip › Modify › Audio Channels, or apply the Fill Left with Right or Fill Right with Left effect. In Audition, Edit › Convert Sample Type converts a file to mono. In Audacity, choose Split Stereo to Mono from the track’s menu (the track name), then delete the empty track.

File formats

FormatTypeUse it for
WAVUncompressedRecording, editing, and sending to other editors. The standard.
AIFFUncompressedSame as WAV. Common on Macs.
MP3LossyPodcast episodes and anything that has to play everywhere
AAC (.m4a)LossySame uses as MP3, slightly better quality at the same size. iPhone recordings and Apple apps.
FLACLosslessMusic archives. Smaller than WAV, same quality. Not all editing software opens it.

Record and edit in WAV or AIFF. Export MP3 or AAC only at the end. Each time a lossy file is edited and exported again, quality drops. Lossy and lossless are explained on Graphics.

Bitrate

For MP3 and AAC, the bitrate sets quality and file size. Higher is better and bigger.

  • Voice, mono: 96–128 kbps
  • Voice with music, stereo: 128–192 kbps
  • Music: 256–320 kbps

Session files

A multitrack project, such as an Audition session (.sesx) or a Premiere project, doesn't contain the audio. It points to the audio files. Keep the audio files in the project folder and don't rename or move them, or the session can't find them. This works like linked images in a layout. See Linked images. An Audacity project (.aup3) is different: it holds the audio inside one file, so it can get very large.

Editing

Destructive and non-destructive editing

  • Destructive: the change is written into the audio file. Audition's Waveform view works this way. Save a copy before you start.
  • Non-destructive: the change is stored in the project, and the original file is untouched. Audition's Multitrack view and Premiere Pro work this way.
  • Audacity does both. Effects from the Effect menu are written into the audio. Effects added in a track's Effects panel, called realtime effects, can be changed or removed later.

Build podcasts and stories in Multitrack. Use Waveform for repairs to a single file. In Audacity, use realtime effects while you're still deciding.

Order of work

  1. Cut the story. Choose and arrange the tape first. Don't polish sound you'll delete.
  2. Clean up. Remove noise, hum, and clicks. Use the lightest setting that works. Heavy noise reduction makes voices sound watery and robotic.
  3. EQ. A high-pass filter at about 80 Hz removes rumble from voices. Make other changes small.
  4. Compression. Evens out loud and quiet words so the listener doesn't reach for the volume.
  5. Mix. Set music and ambient sound well under the voice. If you have to strain to hear words, the music is too loud.
  6. Loudness. Bring the whole piece to the target for where it's going. See Loudness.

Where to find it: In Audition, noise reduction is under Effects › Noise Reduction/Restoration. In Premiere Pro, the Essential Sound panel has Repair, Clarity, and Enhance Speech settings. In Audacity, Effect › Noise Removal and Repair › Noise Reduction: select room tone, choose Get Noise Profile, then select the whole track and apply.

AI speech enhancement

Enhance Speech in Premiere Pro and similar tools rebuild a voice to sound like a studio recording. They can rescue bad audio. They can also change how a voice sounds, add artifacts, and remove the room so it no longer matches the video. Use them at partial strength and compare with the original.

Editing standards

In journalism, edits can't change what a person meant.

  • Allowed: removing ums, pauses, false starts, and repeated words, when the meaning stays the same. Tightening for time.
  • Not allowed: joining parts of different answers so they sound like one, moving an answer to a different question, or adding sound that wasn't there and presenting it as real.
  • Disclose re-created scenes, sound effects, and music added to reported scenes.

Strategic communication has more room, but quoted people should still sound like what they said. Get approval when a quote is edited heavily.

Loudness

Peak levels show the loudest instant. They don't tell you how loud something sounds. LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale) measures loudness as people hear it, averaged over the whole piece. Platforms use it to set every file to about the same volume.

DestinationLoudness targetPeak limit
Podcasts−16 LUFS−1 dBTP
YouTube, Spotify, social video−14 LUFS−1 dBTP
U.S. broadcast TV−24 LKFS−2 dBTP
Video for class or a website−16 to −14 LUFS−1 dBTP

LKFS is the same measurement as LUFS under another name. dBTP (true peak) is a peak measurement that also catches peaks between samples.

Too quiet sounds unprofessional next to other podcasts. Too loud gets turned down by the platform and can sound squashed.

Where to find it: In Audition, Window › Match Loudness measures and sets loudness. In Premiere Pro, the export settings have a Loudness Normalization option under Effects. In Audacity, Effect › Volume and Compression › Loudness Normalization. Apply a limiter at −1 dB to catch the last peaks.

Podcast episodes

A podcast host stores the files and makes the feed that apps like Apple Podcasts and Spotify read. Each episode needs:

  • An episode title that says what it's about. Apps cut titles off, so put the important words first.
  • Show notes: a short description, guests' names, links mentioned, and credits for music.
  • Cover art for the show: a square image, 3000 × 3000 px, JPG or PNG, RGB. It shows tiny in apps, so test it at thumbnail size. See Viewing at size.

Check on small speakers

Most people hear your work on earbuds, a phone speaker, or a laptop. Before you hand it in, listen on at least one of those, not only on studio headphones. Low voices and quiet music can disappear on a phone speaker.

Transcripts and accessibility

  • Post a transcript with every podcast episode and audio story. People who are deaf or hard of hearing need it. It also makes the episode searchable.
  • Label speakers by name, and note important sounds and music in brackets: [laughter], [phone rings], [upbeat music].
  • Check automatic transcripts. Premiere Pro, Apple Podcasts, and podcast hosts make them, but they misspell names and places and get words wrong. Read the whole thing against the audio before publishing.
  • Audio in video needs captions. See Captions and accessibility.

Where to find it: In Premiere Pro, the Text panel's Transcript tab transcribes a sequence and exports the text.

Quick answers

Why does my recording sound distorted?
The level was too high and the recording clipped. It usually can't be fixed. Record again with peaks around −12 dBFS. See Levels and clipping.
Why does my recording sound echoey or far away?
The mic was too far from the person, or the room has hard surfaces. Move the mic closer and record in a softer room. See Mic placement.
Why is my audio only in one ear?
The mic was recorded to one channel of a stereo file. Set the clip to play that channel in both ears. See Mono and stereo.
Why is my podcast quieter than everyone else's?
It hasn't been brought to a loudness target. Set it to −16 LUFS. See Loudness.
Why does my voice sound robotic after cleanup?
The noise reduction or speech enhancement is too strong. Use a lighter setting, and record in a quieter room next time. See Editing.
Why can't my session find the audio files?
The files were moved or renamed. Keep them in the project folder. See Session files.
Can I use this song?
Probably not without a license. See Rights and Credit.
