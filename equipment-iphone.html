iPhone Camera

What the iPhone camera is doing when you only point and shoot, and how to take control of it: lenses, zoom, aperture, pixel dimensions, focus and exposure, computational photography, and file formats.

Exposure, focal length, and depth of field are explained on Photo. This page applies them to the iPhone. Menu paths are for iOS 27 (fall 2026). Older versions put a few settings in different places.

A phone in auto mode makes every choice for you: where to focus, how bright, how much to sharpen, what color the light is. Those choices are usually fine for a snapshot. Professional work needs you to make them on purpose, and to know what the phone changed so you can stand behind the photo.

Which iPhone you have

The features on this page depend on the model. Check yours before you read the tables.

Where to find it: Settings › General › About. The model is next to Model Name.

There are three kinds of iPhone camera setups:

  • Pro models (iPhone 12 Pro and later Pro and Pro Max): three lenses, including a telephoto. Only Pro models shoot ProRAW.
  • Standard models (iPhone 12 through 17, Plus and mini, and iPhone Duo): two lenses, ultra wide and main.
  • Single-lens models (iPhone Air, 16e, 17e, and SE): one main lens, no ultra wide.

Lenses and focal lengths

Each iPhone has two or three separate cameras, each with its own lens and sensor. The buttons in the Camera app (0.5×, 1×, 2×, and so on) switch between them. Some buttons don't switch to a new lens. They crop the middle of the main sensor instead. The table marks those as crop.

Apple lists focal lengths as full-frame equivalents: the focal length that would give the same view on a full-frame camera. That lets you compare a phone with any camera. The real focal lengths are only a few millimeters, because the sensors are tiny. See Focal length and lenses for what each range is good for.

What each lens is for

  • 0.5× ultra wide (13 mm): small rooms, buildings, big crowds from inside. It stretches anything near the edges and makes faces close to it look distorted. Keep people toward the middle of the frame, and don't use it for close portraits.
  • 1× main (24–26 mm): the best camera on every iPhone. It has the biggest sensor and the widest aperture, so it handles low light best. Use it whenever you can.
  • 2× (48–52 mm): close to a standard 50 mm lens. Good for portraits of people and for cutting clutter out of the frame. A face looks more natural at 2× from a few steps back than at 1× up close.
  • 3×, 4×, 5× telephoto (77–120 mm): head-and-shoulders portraits, speakers on a stage, details, birds at the feeder. The telephoto lens has a smaller sensor and lets in less light, so photos get noisy indoors and at night.

Choosing 24, 28, or 35 mm for the 1× button

On recent models, the 1× button can also shoot at 28 mm and 35 mm. These are crops from the main sensor. 35 mm is a classic documentary and street focal length: a little tighter than 24 mm, with less stretching at the edges.

Where to find it: Settings › Camera › Main Camera (called Fusion Camera on some models). Turn on the extra lenses under Additional Lenses and choose a Default Lens. In the Camera app, tap the 1× button to cycle through them. Photo resolution must be set to 24 MP (see Pixel dimensions).

Editor note: confirm which models have this setting. Apple's spec pages list it for the Pro models, Air, and Duo. Not confirmed for the base iPhone 17, 16, or 15.

Zoom: optical and digital

There are three kinds of zoom on an iPhone, and only one of them is free.

KindWhat it doesQuality
Optical (a separate lens)Switches to the ultra wide or telephoto cameraFull quality for that camera
Optical-quality cropUses the middle 12 MP of the 48 MP main sensor (the 2× button on most recent models, 8× on 17 Pro and 18 Pro)Good in daylight. A 12 MP photo instead of 24 or 48 MP, and a little more noise in low light.
Digital zoomCrops the image and enlarges what's left to fill the frameLoses detail the further you go. The phone fills in with sharpening and processing, which can look smeared or painted.

Any zoom setting that isn't on a lens button is digital zoom. Pinching to 1.7× crops the 1× camera, and 7× on a 17 Pro crops the 4× camera. Maximum digital zoom runs from 5× on the iPhone 12 through 14 to 40× on the 17 Pro and 18 Pro.

  • Tap a lens button instead of pinching. You'll always be on a real lens or the 2× crop.
  • Walk closer when you can. Moving your feet costs no pixels.
  • Don't zoom digitally for news photos you might need to enlarge. You can crop later from the full-quality file, but you can't get back detail that digital zoom threw away. See Cropping costs pixels.
  • In low light, step back to 1×. In dim light the phone sometimes uses the main camera and crops it even when you tapped the telephoto, because the main camera lets in more light. The photo still says 3× or 5×, but it's a digital crop.

Aperture and depth of field

On most iPhones, aperture can't be changed. Each lens has one fixed f-number. The exception is the iPhone 18 Pro (below).

Why phone photos have so much in focus

The f-number on a phone means the same thing for brightness as it does on any camera: f/1.78 lets in as much light per area of the sensor as f/1.78 on a big camera. But depth of field depends on the size of the sensor too. A phone's main sensor is about a quarter to a third the width of a full-frame sensor, so the same f-number gives much deeper focus.

To compare depth of field with a full-frame camera, multiply the f-number by the crop factor. This is the full-frame equivalent aperture. It tells you about blur only, not brightness.

The ultra wide and telephoto cameras have even smaller sensors, so their depth of field is deeper still. In practice: an iPhone can blur the background only when the subject is very close, like a flower or a coffee cup. A person standing a few feet away will have a mostly sharp background unless the phone adds blur in software (Portrait mode or the Depth button).

Background blur is simulated

Background blur on an iPhone doesn't change the aperture. The phone measures depth, guesses where the subject ends, and blurs the rest in software. It can do this in two places:

  • Portrait mode (swipe to Portrait): every photo gets blur.
  • Photo mode, on iPhone 15 and later (not the 16e): when the camera sees a face, a Depth button appears with the other controls. When the button is yellow, the photo is saved with blur. Tap it so it isn't yellow to shoot without blur. Either way, the phone saves depth information, so you can add or remove blur later.

Watch for the yellow Depth button in Photo mode. It's easy to add blur to a photo without meaning to.

The Depth control sets how much blur is added. It's labeled with f-numbers from f/1.4 to f/16, so it looks like an aperture setting, but it isn't one. The lens stays at its one fixed aperture, and brightness, shutter speed, and noise don't change. The numbers only copy what that aperture would look like on a full-frame camera:

  • f/1.4: the most blur. Mistakes at the edges show most here.
  • f/16: almost no added blur.
  • Something around f/4 to f/5.6 often looks more natural and hides edge mistakes.

Where to find it: Tap Depth in the camera controls (if you don't see it, tap the six dots or swipe up from the mode names), then drag the slider. With the Camera Control button, lightly press twice and choose Depth. To change or remove the blur after shooting, open the photo in Photos, tap Edit, then Portrait: drag the slider, or tap Portrait to turn the blur off.

  • Check the edges: hair, glasses, fingers, and the gaps between arms and the body are where it goes wrong.
  • On iPhone 13 and later you can also change the focus point after you shoot, in the same Portrait editing screen.
  • For news photos, follow your outlet's rules. Many treat added blur as an alteration, like blurring in Photoshop. See Editing standards.

Setting the aperture on iPhone 18 Pro

The iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max main camera has a real adjustable aperture with four stops: f/1.48, f/1.8, f/2.8, and f/4. It only applies to the 1× and 2× buttons; the other lenses are fixed.

  • Auto is the default. The phone picks the aperture.
  • f/1.48 lets in the most light and gives the shallowest focus, but it's a little soft at the edges.
  • f/1.8 is a good everyday setting.
  • f/2.8 and f/4 keep more in focus, for groups of people at different distances, and sharper details. f/4 lets in a quarter of the light of f/1.8, so the phone needs a slower shutter or higher ISO.

Where to find it: In the Camera app, tap the Camera Controls button (six dots), then Lens Aperture, and tap a stop. To keep your choice the next time you open Camera, turn on Settings › Camera › Preserve Settings › Lens Aperture. The same menu on the 18 Pro has shutter speed, white balance, and a live histogram. See Histograms and clipping.

  • 48 MP doesn't mean four times the detail of 12 MP. The phone's 48 MP sensor groups pixels in fours to gather light, and in daylight it rebuilds a full 48 MP image from them. You get more detail in good light, much less gain in dim light, and files several times larger.
  • Use 48 MP when you need to crop heavily or print large, in good light. Use 24 MP for everyday work.

Where to find it: Settings › Camera › Formats › Photo Mode, choose 12 MP or 24 MP. For 48 MP, turn on Resolution Control (or ProRAW & Resolution Control on Pro models) in the same menu, then choose the size in the Camera app with the format button or the six-dot menu.

Focus and exposure

In auto, the iPhone picks a focus point (usually a face, or whatever is closest) and sets the brightness for the whole scene. Both are easy to take back.

Tap to focus

Tap the screen on what matters. A yellow box shows where the phone is focusing. For people and animals, tap the nearest eye. Tapping also sets the brightness for that spot, so tapping a dark face in front of a bright window brightens the photo.

Lock focus and exposure

Touch and hold on your subject until AE/AF Lock appears at the top of the screen. Focus and brightness now stay put while you recompose, wait for a moment, or shoot a series. Without the lock, the phone readjusts every time something moves through the frame. Tap the screen anywhere to unlock.

Use it when:

  • You're waiting for something to happen in one spot, like a runner sliding into second base.
  • You're shooting several photos of the same scene that need to match in brightness.
  • The subject is off-center and the phone keeps focusing on the background.

Brighten or darken

After you tap to focus, a sun icon appears beside the yellow box. Drag up to brighten, down to darken. This is exposure compensation: it tells the phone you want the photo brighter or darker than it chose. See Exposure compensation.

  • Darken for bright skies, stage lights, and sunsets. Phones often overexpose bright things. Dragging down until the bright areas show detail is the single most useful habit for better phone photos.
  • Brighten for snow, white walls, and backlit people.

The sun slider resets when you tap somewhere new. For a setting that stays, use the Exposure control in the Camera Controls menu (six dots). It holds until you close the Camera app, or until you change it if Settings › Camera › Preserve Settings › Exposure Adjustment is on. Remember to set it back to 0.

Camera Control button

iPhone 16 and later (not the 16e or 17e) have a Camera Control button on the lower right side.

  • Click to open the camera. Click again to take a photo. Click and hold to record video.
  • Lightly press twice to open a settings menu. Slide your finger along the button to scroll to Exposure, Depth, Zoom, Cameras, Styles, or Tone, lightly press to pick one, then slide to adjust it.
  • Lock focus and exposure with the button: turn on Settings › Camera › Camera Control › Lock Focus and Exposure. Then lightly press and hold. The lock lasts only while your finger stays on the button.

Where to find it: Choose which settings appear, and in what order, in Settings › Camera › Camera Control › Customize.

Manual focus and other controls on newer Pro models

On iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone Duo, focus and exposure are separate controls, so you can focus on one spot and set brightness from another. Double-tap the yellow focus box to track a moving subject. The 18 Pro also has shutter speed and white balance controls in the six-dot menu. On older models, a third-party camera app is the way to get manual shutter speed, ISO, and focus (see Getting around the processing).

Burst, the volume buttons, and holding steady

  • Burst: drag the shutter button to the left and hold. The phone shoots until you let go. Good for sports, birds taking off, and anything you can't time. To pick the keepers, open the burst in Photos and tap the Burst label at the top. To use the volume-up button for bursts instead, turn on Settings › Camera › Use Volume Up for Burst.
  • Volume buttons work as a shutter button. Pressing a side button shakes the phone less than tapping the screen, especially held sideways with two hands.
  • Hold it with two hands, elbows against your body. Phones use slow shutter speeds indoors, and the shake shows as blur. Lean on a wall or set the phone on something steady when the light is dim.
  • Wipe the lens. A fingerprint on the lens is the most common cause of hazy, glowing phone photos. Use a soft cloth or the inside of your shirt.

Computational photography

An iPhone photo isn't one exposure. When you press the shutter, the phone has already been saving frames. It combines several of them, at different brightnesses, then picks out faces, sky, and skin, and processes each area differently: brightening shadows, darkening the sky, smoothing noise, sharpening edges. This is computational photography. It's why a tiny sensor can take a good photo of a backlit person.

It also has costs a professional should notice:

  • Flat light: shadows lifted and highlights pulled down until everything has the same brightness. Faces lose shape, and sunsets look gray.
  • Oversharpening: hair, grass, and brick look crunchy or painted at 100%.
  • Smearing: fine detail in low light turns to watercolor.
  • Halos: bright outlines where a dark subject meets a bright sky.
  • Changed color: skies bluer, skin smoother, and grass greener than they were.

Look at a phone photo at 100% before you use it in professional work. See Noise reduction and sharpening: phone photos are already sharpened, so add little or none.

Night mode

Night mode turns dark scenes bright and clean, but it needs everything to hold still for one or more seconds. Moving people turn into blurs or ghosts, and the photo can look brighter than the scene really was. For a news photo of a night vigil, the bright Night mode version may not show what the scene looked like. Turn it off when the subject moves or when the darkness is part of the story.

Live Photos

A Live Photo saves a photo and a short video together. They take up more space, some apps and websites save only the still, and the motion before and after the shutter can record conversations. Turn Live off for professional work unless you want the motion. Live Photos also can't be shot with ProRAW or 48 MP.

Photographic Styles

Styles add a look to every photo before you see it. Standard has no added look. The others change tone and color: Rose Gold, Amber, Cool, and so on. Styles are fine for personal photos, but for professional work you want to choose the look while editing, for each photo. Set Standard as the default.

Where to find it: Settings › Camera › Photographic Styles, choose Standard. To keep the Camera app from switching back to the last style you used, turn off Settings › Camera › Preserve Settings › Photographic Style.

Editing tools in Photos

Edits in the Photos app don't change the original. To go back, open the photo, tap More (•••), then Revert to Original.

Clean Up in the Photos app uses AI to erase people and objects and invent what was behind them. It's generative, not a retouch. Don't use it on news photos; it changes what the photo shows. For marketing work, say what was removed if anyone could be misled. See Editing standards and AI.

Getting around the processing

  • ProRAW doesn't turn it off. A ProRAW file still includes Smart HDR, Deep Fusion, and Night mode merging. You get much more room to edit color and brightness, but not an untouched photo. See File formats.
  • Third-party camera apps can skip some or all of Apple's processing and add manual controls (shutter speed, ISO, focus, and a histogram):
    • Halide (paid) has a Process Zero mode that skips Apple's processing. Photos are 12 MP and noisier in low light, but natural.
    • Project Indigo (free, from Adobe) uses its own multi-frame processing with a more natural look and can save RAW files.
    • Lightroom mobile (free with an Adobe account) has a built-in camera with manual controls.
  • Expose for the highlights. Darkening with the sun slider gives the phone less to rescue and usually cuts down the flat, HDR look.

HEIC or JPG

Keep High Efficiency (HEIC) unless HEIC causes problems for you. Most current software opens it: Photoshop, Lightroom, Canva, and current Windows with Apple's free extensions. iPhones also convert to JPG on their own in many cases: when you AirDrop, email, or message to a device that can't open HEIC, and when you copy to a computer with the transfer setting on Automatic.

Switch to Most Compatible (JPG) if you often upload straight from your phone to a website or app that rejects HEIC, or you hand files to a Windows computer without Apple's extensions.

Where to find it: Settings › Camera › Formats, choose High Efficiency or Most Compatible. This only affects new photos. For photos already taken, export as JPG: in the Photos app, tap Share, then Options, and choose Most Compatible.

Copying photos to a computer

  • By cable, the phone converts HEIC to JPG on the way unless you change it. To copy the originals, choose Settings › Apps › Photos › Transfer to Mac or PC › Keep Originals.
  • On a Mac, use AirDrop or the Photos app. To get the original file with no edits, select the photo in Photos on the Mac and choose File › Export › Export Unmodified Original.
  • On Windows, install Apple's free Apple Devices app, and the HEIF and HEVC extensions from the Microsoft Store if HEIC files won't open.
  • Don't send professional photos by text message. Messages can shrink them. Use AirDrop, a cable, or a cloud folder.

Shooting ProRAW

  1. Turn on Settings › Camera › Formats › ProRAW & Resolution Control.
  2. In the Camera app, tap the format button at the top of the screen and choose RAW (12 MP) or RAW Max (48 MP).
  3. To keep it on, turn on Settings › Camera › Preserve Settings › ProRAW & Resolution Control.

ProRAW doesn't work with Live Photos, Portrait mode, or video. RAW Max works only on the 1× main camera; every other lens, Night mode, flash, and macro save 12 MP. Files open in Lightroom, Photoshop (through Camera Raw), and the Photos app. ProRAW photos look flatter than HEIC straight out of the camera. That's expected: they're meant to be edited.

Editor note: newer Pro models let you save ProRAW as JPEG-XL (smaller files) under Settings › Camera › Formats › ProRAW Format. Confirm the option names on an iOS 27 phone, and whether to recommend one.

Location in shared photos

Each photo saves where it was taken, and that travels with the file when you share it. Before sharing a photo that could reveal a source's home or a private place, remove the location: open the photo, tap More (•••), then Adjust Info › Adjust Location, and remove it. See Location data.

Editor note: in iOS 26, Share › Options had a Location switch. Apple's iOS 27 guide no longer lists it. Check on an iOS 27 phone before publishing.

Habits that make the biggest difference

  1. Wipe the lens.
  2. Turn on the grid and level, and keep horizons straight.
  3. Tap on your subject, then drag the sun down until the bright areas have detail.
  4. Tap lens buttons instead of pinching. Walk closer instead of zooming.
  5. Shoot 4:3 and crop later.
  6. Hold the phone with two hands and press a volume button.
  7. Check the photo at 100% before you use it.

Quick answers

Why is my zoomed photo blurry or painted-looking?
You used digital zoom, or the light was too dim for the telephoto and the phone cropped the main camera. Tap a lens button, or walk closer. See Zoom.
Why does the sky look gray and flat?
Smart HDR pulled the sky down and the shadows up. Tap the sky and drag the sun icon down, then brighten your subject while editing. See Computational photography.
Why does everything in my photo have a glow or haze?
There's a fingerprint on the lens. Wipe it.
Why does my phone keep switching lenses when I get close?
Macro is switching to the ultra wide camera. Tap the flower button to turn it off, or step back. See Macro.
Why can't I get a blurry background without adding blur in software?
The sensor is small, so depth of field is deep. Get very close to the subject, use the 2× or telephoto button, or use Portrait mode or the Depth button and check the edges. See Aperture and depth of field.
Why won't my photo upload or open?
It's HEIC. Export it as JPG with Share › Options › Most Compatible, or switch the camera to Most Compatible. See File formats.
Why are people blurry in my night photos?
Night mode held the shutter open for a second or more. Tap the moon button to turn it off. See Night mode.
Why is the background blurry in a photo I took in Photo mode?
The Depth button was on (yellow). Remove the blur in Photos › Edit › Portrait, and tap Depth off before your next shot. See Background blur is simulated.
Is the f/1.4 to f/16 Depth setting the aperture?
No. It sets how much background blur the phone adds in software, in Portrait mode or in Photo mode when the Depth button is yellow. Only the iPhone 18 Pro has an adjustable aperture, and it uses a separate Lens Aperture control. See Aperture and depth of field.
Why is my selfie backwards?
Mirror Front Camera is on. Turn it off in Settings › Camera, or flip the photo while editing.
Which iPhone do I have?
Settings › General › About › Model Name.
