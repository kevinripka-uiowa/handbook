Research

How to find information you can trust, check it, and credit it, for news stories, campaigns, graphics, and anything else you publish.

Every fact in your work should trace back to a real source you found, opened, and read. The rest of this page is how.

Three ways to know something

  1. "ChatGPT told me." You have an answer, but you don't know where it came from.
  2. "I found three articles." Better, but all three may repeat one source. See The information laundering chain.
  3. "I read the original." You found the study, data, or record, understood what was measured, and can explain what it shows and what it doesn't.

Only the third one is knowing it. AI makes it possible to skip the research and still turn in something that looks finished. Your editor, client, and audience are trusting that you did the third one.

Kinds of sources

KindWhat it isExamples
PrimaryFirsthand: the original record, data, or accountInterviews you did, government data, court records, a study's own report, a company's annual report, original photos and video, meeting minutes
SecondarySomeone else's report or analysis of primary sourcesNews stories, magazine features, books, industry reports that summarize research
TertiarySummaries of secondary sourcesEncyclopedias, Wikipedia, textbooks, fact sheets, generative AI answers

Use tertiary sources to get oriented. Use secondary sources to find out what's known and who knows it. Base your facts on primary sources whenever you can.

Example: a news story says bird populations in Iowa are falling. That's secondary. The study it's based on, with the data and methods, is primary. Read the study. The story may have left out which birds, which years, or how sure the researchers are.

Match the source to the claim

There's no single best kind of source, only the best source for what you're claiming.

If you're claimingLook for
A research findingThe study itself, not a story about it
A recent eventNews with editors and named reporters
A statisticThe agency or group that collected the data
What an expert thinksThat expert's own article, talk, or blog
What someone said or didThe original record: the post, transcript, recording, or document

A social media post can be the right source, if your claim is that the person posted it.

The UI Libraries pay for databases and reports that cost money elsewhere. Sign in with your HawkID. Librarians help with research questions for free.

Searching well

  • Use quotation marks for exact phrases: "Iowa Cubs" attendance.
  • Limit to a site or type of site: site:gov bird survey, site:uiowa.edu.
  • Limit to a file type for reports: filetype:pdf annual report.
  • Check the date of everything you find. Use search tools to limit results to recent years.
  • Search for the original. When a source cites a number or a study, search for that study, not more stories about it.

Wikipedia

Wikipedia is a good place to start and a bad place to stop. It summarizes other sources. It doesn't do its own research.

Using it isn't citing it

  • Use it to learn the basics of a topic, find the right terms to search, and find sources in its footnotes. Basic facts like dates, names, places, and definitions are usually reliable there.
  • Don't cite it for research findings, statistics, causes and effects, controversies, or anything your work depends on. Wikipedia's own guidance says to cite its sources instead. Anyone can edit it, and an article can change between when you read it and when someone checks.
  • Cite it only when Wikipedia itself is your topic, you quoted or paraphrased it, or nothing else exists and your instructor or editor agrees. Use the permanent link (Tools › Permanent link) so readers see the version you saw.

Cite what Wikipedia cites

  1. Find the claim you want to use.
  2. Click its footnote.
  3. Open the source and read it.
  4. Check that it says what Wikipedia says it does.
  5. Cite that source.

No footnote? Find a real source or drop the claim. Never copy a reference you haven't opened. Footnotes can also lead back to Wikipedia itself. See When Wikipedia launders its own errors.

AI is not a search engine

A search engine retrieves. It points to pages that already exist, and you open them, see who wrote them, and judge them yourself. Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini generate. They predict what a likely answer sounds like. Usually that's close to true. Sometimes it's confidently wrong, and the two look the same.

What goes wrong

  • Invented sources. They produce citations, links, studies, and quotes that don't exist, with real-sounding authors and journals.
  • Real sources, wrong claims. A real study is cited for something it doesn't say.
  • Wrong details. Dates, numbers, names, and titles are slightly off.
  • Out of date. Training data stops at a point in time. Recent events may be missing or mixed up.
  • Web search doesn't fix it. See the next section.

How often this happens, and the terms for it, are on AI: Accuracy.

When AI searches the web

Many AI tools now search the web or read a file you upload, then write an answer from what they found, with links. This is called retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). It helps, but the facts still pass through a language model that rewrites them. It doesn't copy and paste. Three things go wrong:

ErrorWhat happens
Retrieval errorIt finds bad, outdated, or poorly ranked sources, then summarizes them faithfully.
Interpretation errorIt finds the right source and misreads it: a chart, a double negative, two facts merged into one.
OverwriteWhat it expects to be true wins out over the source in front of it.

A link next to an answer means the tool found a page. It doesn't mean the page says what the answer says. Open it.

AI summaries in search results

Google and other search engines now put an AI-written summary above the results. It looks like a search result, but it's generated. In a Pew Research Center study of 900 U.S. adults' browsing in March 2025, people clicked a search result on 15% of visits with no AI summary and 8% with one. They clicked a link inside the summary on 1%. Scroll past the summary and open the sources.

Use it on topics you know

When you read about something you know well, you spot the errors. Then you trust the same source on a topic you don't know. Writer Michael Crichton called this the Gell-Mann Amnesia effect. AI does it to everyone: on your own subject you can see where it's wrong, and on everything else it sounds right.

  • Use AI mostly on topics you already know well.
  • On a new topic, use it for words to search with and a rough map of the subject. It can miss nuance and repeat popular myths, because it learned from what's on the web.
  • Verify everything else.

How to use it

  • To get oriented: explain a term or a process you'll then check in a real source.
  • To find search terms for library databases, angles, and kinds of experts to look for.
  • To raise counterarguments that test your thinking.
  • To summarize a long document you also read yourself.

Checking a source AI gives you

  1. Does it exist? Search the title in the UI Libraries search or Google Scholar.
  2. Are the details right? Check the authors, year, publication, and page numbers.
  3. Does it say that? Open it and read the passage. Real sources get misdescribed too.
  4. Is it any good? Existing doesn't make it credible. See Checking a source.

The rule

AI is never the source. Every fact, number, and quote in your work has to come from a real source you opened and read: a document, a dataset, a person you interviewed. If you didn't read it, you can't cite it. Don't cite an AI tool as a source of facts.

Try it

Ask a generative AI tool for five scholarly sources on a topic you know well. Run the four checks above on each one. Sort them into three groups: real and accurate, real but misdescribed, and not found. Notice which kind of error fooled you longest.

The information laundering chain

Information changes every time someone summarizes it.

  1. A researcher publishes a study with specific findings and limits.
  2. A journalist summarizes the study and leaves out some of the detail.
  3. A website summarizes the journalist's story.
  4. An AI tool summarizes that website and others like it.
  5. A student asks the AI and publishes the answer.

At each step, limits and conditions drop out, a link between two things becomes one causing the other, and a finding about one group becomes a claim about everyone. The information gets more polished as it gets less accurate.

Example: a study finds that people who keep bird feeders report less stress. A news story says bird feeders reduce stress. A website says birdwatching cures anxiety. Each step sounds more certain than the study.

The chain also multiplies. Five websites repeating one claim aren't five confirmations if all five trace back to the same misread study. Follow the claim back to the first link in the chain and read that.

When Wikipedia launders its own errors

In 2009, a Wikipedia article gave the release year of the Casio F-91W watch as 1991. It was 1989. In 2011, the BBC repeated 1991, so the wrong date now had a respected source behind it, which made it harder to correct. It was fixed in 2019, after a news report pointed out the loop. This is called circular reporting, or citogenesis. A source that looks independent may have gotten its fact from Wikipedia. Check its date and where it got the fact.

Checking a source

Books, journals, and newspapers pass through editors and reviewers before you see them. Most of the web and social media doesn't. That checking now happens after publication, and it's your job. Good web sources still have editors or peers behind them. Part of checking is finding out whether they do.

Professional fact-checkers don't judge a website by how it looks. They leave it and read about it somewhere else. This is called lateral reading.

  1. Stop. Before you use or share it, ask whether you know who's behind it.
  2. Investigate the source. Open a new tab and search for the organization or author. Who funds it? What do others say about it?
  3. Find better coverage. Search for the claim itself. Do reliable sources report the same thing?
  4. Trace it to the original. Follow quotes, numbers, photos, and studies back to where they first appeared.

These four steps are known as SIFT, from fact-checking educator Mike Caulfield.

In a 2017 Stanford study, professional fact-checkers who read this way were faster and more accurate than PhD historians and Stanford students. The historians and students read down the page and were often fooled by logos, references, and a .org address. Being smart isn't the protection. The technique is, and anyone can learn it.

Lateral reading in one minute

  1. Don't keep reading the page. Open a new tab.
  2. Search the author or organization by name.
  3. Add a word like funding, bias, or criticism.
  4. Scan the results before you click anything.
  5. Decide whether the source deserves your time.

Wikipedia is fine for this step. You're finding out who runs something, not citing it.

Signals that prove nothing

  • A polished design or logo
  • A .org or .edu address
  • A confident About page
  • A long list of references
  • A lot of information

Anyone can buy a .org address and write an About page.

Questions for any source

  • Who made it, and what do they know about this?
  • Why does it exist? To inform, persuade, sell, or raise money? Advocacy groups and companies publish real data, but choose what to show.
  • When was it published or updated?
  • Where does the information come from? Does it show its sources?

When a blog is a good source

The format isn't the test. A blog or newsletter can be a good source if it passes these:

TestWhat to look for
A real authorNamed, with a record you can confirm somewhere else
ExpertiseTraining or experience in this exact subject
EvidenceLinks to data and original sources you can follow
AccountabilityCorrections when it's wrong, and disclosed funding
Respect from peersOther experts cite or discuss the work

Examples in our field include Nieman Lab, Poynter, Retraction Watch, and Nielsen Norman Group. Run the tests on them yourself. When a good blog links to a study, follow the link and cite what you read.

Checking images and video

  • Reverse image search. Upload the image or paste its link to Google Lens or TinEye to find where it appeared first and whether it's been changed. Old photos often circulate as new.
  • Check video by its frames. Take screenshots of key frames and reverse-search them.
  • Look for the original poster. Check the account's history and location. Contact them to confirm they took it and to ask permission. See Social media posts.
  • Check details against the claim. Weather, signs, shadows, license plates, landmarks, and clothing should fit the place and date.
  • "Enhanced" isn't more accurate. AI upscaling and enhancing invent detail based on other images. In 2025, an AI-enhanced version of a grainy Getty photo of President Trump spread online with details that weren't in the original. Sharper doesn't mean truer. Find the original file. See AI: Kinds of AI tools.
  • Check content credentials when a file has them. See AI: Content credentials.
  • Don't rely on spotting AI by eye. AI images and video are getting harder to spot. Odd hands or text are clues, but a clean image proves nothing. Tracing it to the source is more reliable.

Checking numbers

  • Find the original study or dataset, not a story about it.
  • Check who paid for it and who collected the data.
  • Check the date, place, and group the number applies to. "40% of fans" means which fans, where, and when?
  • Check the size. A survey of 50 people says less than a survey of 5,000. A jump from 2 to 4 is a 100% increase but only two more.
  • Percent and number aren't the same. Give both when one alone could mislead.
  • Do the math yourself. Recalculate percentages and changes before you put them in a chart or a headline.

Attribution and citation

Your audience should be able to tell where every fact came from.

WorkHow to credit sources
News storiesAttribute in the text: "according to the Iowa Department of Natural Resources," "said Maria Lopez, a park ranger." Link to documents and data online.
Graphics and chartsA source line under the graphic: "Source: U.S. Census Bureau, 2024 American Community Survey."
Video and audioSay it in the narration or put it on screen. List sources in the description or show notes.
Reports, white papers, and campaign plansFootnotes or endnotes with a full citation. Follow the style your class or employer uses.
Social postsName the source in the post, or link to it.
Web pagesLink to the source with link text that names it. See Writing for the web.

Using other people's words or ideas without credit is plagiarism, even when it's legal. Credit for photos, music, and other media is on Rights and Credit.

Citing AI

Cite what you actually used. An AI answer is a poor source of facts: no one else can get the same answer, and no one stands behind it. For facts, cite the original source you read. Then say how you used AI, following your class, newsroom, or client rules. See AI: Disclosure and labels.

Style guides are still working this out. APA lists the tool's company as the author. MLA doesn't treat AI as an author. Check the current guidance for the style you use.

Keeping track

Keep a source log for every project, from the start. You'll need it to fact-check, to answer an editor or client, and to write credits.

  • For documents: title, author or organization, date, link, and the date you read it.
  • For people: name, title, contact information, date of the interview, and whether you recorded it.
  • For data: where you downloaded it, the date, and any changes you made.

Save copies of important web pages and documents, because pages change and disappear. Save a PDF, or use the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) to save a dated copy. Where to keep it all is on Files.

Quick answers

Can I cite Wikipedia?
Usually not. Use it to find the original sources in its footnotes, and cite those. See Wikipedia.
Can I use facts that generative AI gave me?
Only after you find each one in a real source you've read. AI is never the source. See AI is not a search engine.
The AI answer had links. Is that enough?
No. Open each link and check that the page says what the answer says. See When AI searches the web.
Five websites say the same thing. Is it confirmed?
Not if they all trace back to one source. Find the original. See The information laundering chain.
The study the AI cited doesn't come up in search. What now?
It probably doesn't exist. Search the library databases for real research on the topic. See Where to search.
How do I know if a website is reliable?
Leave it and search for who runs it and what others say. See Checking a source.
How do I know if a photo is real?
Reverse-search it and trace it to whoever took it. See Checking images and video.
How do I credit a source in a chart?
Put a source line under it. See Attribution and citation.
